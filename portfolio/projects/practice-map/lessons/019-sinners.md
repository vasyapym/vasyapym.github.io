# *Sinners* (2025): the story, the ideas, and the craft

**The short version:** Ryan Coogler’s *Sinners* is a blues-inflected horror film about two brothers who open a juke joint in 1932 Mississippi. It is also a film about who gets to make a place their own, who profits from another person’s gifts, and whether living forever is the same thing as leaving something behind.

## The essentials, without major spoilers

Twin brothers Smoke and Stack, both played by Michael B. Jordan, return from Chicago to the Mississippi Delta with money and a plan: turn an old sawmill into a juke joint. A *juke joint* was an informal venue for music, dancing, drinking, and gathering—particularly important to Black communities whose access to public space was constrained by segregation. The brothers recruit their gifted young cousin Sammie (Miles Caton), the pianist Delta Slim (Delroy Lindo), and other musicians and neighbors. By nightfall, their new club attracts something more dangerous than an unruly crowd.

The film moves among period drama, musical performance, family tragedy, and vampire horror. Coogler wrote and directed it; Autumn Durald Arkapaw photographed it; Ludwig Göransson composed its score. It is **fiction**, not a biopic, though its setting and music draw on real histories. Expect graphic violence, racial terror, and a story of bereavement.

A useful detail to watch from the beginning is **the door**. The brothers’ club offers a rare space they can organize for their own community. As the night unfolds, deciding who may enter becomes both a practical question and the film’s central moral one.

## The story — **major spoilers from here**

The brothers’ opening night brings together people whose relationships are already strained or unfinished: Smoke and Annie (Wunmi Mosaku), whose infant daughter died; Stack and his former lover Mary (Hailee Steinfeld); and Sammie, a preacher’s son drawn powerfully toward blues music despite his father’s objections.

An Irish vampire named Remmick (Jack O’Connell) hears Sammie play and becomes intent on reaching him. Remmick’s followers cannot simply cross the club’s threshold: they need to be invited in. What follows is a siege in which intimacy and trust become weapons. People who leave the safety of the club are turned; among them are Mary and Stack. Remmick presents vampirism as an invitation into a lasting family, but the offer rests on coercion. He covets Sammie’s extraordinary musical gift.

Smoke and Sammie survive the vampire assault until morning. Daylight does not, however, end the danger. Local Ku Klux Klan members arrive, and Smoke fights them at the cost of his life. As he dies, he sees Annie and their lost child. Coogler makes a harsh point with this sequence: the supernatural predators can be driven away by sunrise; organized racist violence remains.

Decades later, an elderly Sammie—played by the real blues guitarist Buddy Guy—performs in Chicago. The still-vampiric Stack and Mary visit and offer him immortality. He declines. He has lived a human life and made music that will outlast him. **Stay after the credits begin:** this epilogue is essential to the ending.

## What the film is really asking

### 1. Can art survive without its maker being possessed?

In the film’s most exuberant sequence, Sammie’s playing seems to bring musicians from different times into the same room. African diasporic traditions and forms yet to come meet on the dance floor. The scene is not a literal claim that one guitarist invented all subsequent music. It gives visible form to an idea about **inheritance**: a tradition lives because later people receive it, alter it, and pass it on.

That offers one answer to the vampires. Remmick promises a kind of preservation—an endless life together. Sammie’s music offers another: something of a person can continue after death *without* anyone acquiring their body or controlling their gift. The film’s closing refusal makes that distinction matter.

### 2. When does belonging become possession?

Remmick’s promise is seductive for a reason. His followers retain memories, attachments, and an experience of togetherness; Stack and Mary are not rendered into simple, empty monsters. In a world structured by exclusion, the prospect of a family that cannot die has force.

But a community is not made ethical merely by calling itself a family. The juke joint’s best moments depend on people gathering as distinct people; Remmick’s version of unity begins by overriding their choice. **The contrast is not between mixing cultures and keeping them separate. It is between exchange and extraction, invitation and violation.**

### 3. Who gets called a “sinner”?

Sammie’s father regards the blues as spiritually dangerous. The film takes that fear seriously without accepting that pleasure or secular music is its greatest evil. There is criminality in the brothers’ past, and the club is no utopia. Yet its dancers and musicians are not morally equivalent to the people who prey on them.

Annie’s spiritual knowledge complicates any easy division between “church” and “superstition,” or sacred life and musical life. Her conjure practice belongs to a history of African American spiritual work; it should not be casually equated with Haitian Vodou or treated as another name for evil. *Sinners* asks viewers to look past the moral labels placed on people and judge what they actually do to one another.

### 4. Why make the vampire Irish?

Remmick brings songs and memories of his own threatened culture. That choice prevents him from being merely a blank emblem of white appropriation: he understands something about loss. Understanding loss, however, does not entitle him to take Sammie’s gift or absorb Sammie’s community.

This is a more precise argument than “cultural contact is theft.” People can share music across histories. Remmick’s wrong lies in treating another person’s artistry as something he may claim through power. His remembered suffering does not excuse the suffering he imposes.

### 5. Why does the film end with human violence?

The vampires give the night its spectacular horror, but Jim Crow gives the story its enduring conditions. The brothers can buy a building and open a business, yet their control of that space remains precarious. When the Klan appears after the supernatural battle, the film refuses the comforting thought that defeating one monster resolves the world that made the club vulnerable.

That ending also changes how we remember the opening celebration. Its joy was real—not an illusion exposed by the violence. The violence is appalling partly because we have seen what people made together before it arrived.

## How the filmmaking makes those ideas felt

Much of the music is **diegetic**: the characters are playing and hearing it within the story. Coogler then lets performance exceed ordinary realism. In “I Lied to You,” the club seems to hold ancestors and future musicians at once. Instead of pausing for a lecture on musical history, the film lets the room *become* an argument about continuity.

The images matter, too. *Sinners* was shot using IMAX 65mm and Ultra Panavision 70; in compatible presentations, its changing frame can make communal performance feel expansive and the siege feel newly constricted. The film’s genre shifts follow a similar pattern: the daylight work of assembling a business becomes a night of musical possibility, then a battle to defend the people inside it.

For a film geek, the premise may recall *From Dusk Till Dawn*: criminals, a club, and an abrupt turn toward vampires. What distinguishes Coogler’s film is the attention it gives to the club *before* the attack—to its labor, relationships, music, and place in a segregated economy.

## Two useful distinctions between history and myth

Clarksdale and the Mississippi Delta are associated with the legend that bluesman Robert Johnson sold his soul at a crossroads. That is **folklore, not documented history**. *Sinners* uses the legend’s language of devils and musical power to ask why Black virtuosity has so often been described as suspect.

Likewise, the older Sammie is a **fictional character portrayed by Buddy Guy**, not a portrayal of Guy’s own life. The casting nevertheless gives the ending a special resonance: an actual blues musician embodies the passage of music between generations.

**The film’s deepest contrast is between two ways of lasting.** A vampire lives on by taking and holding. Sammie chooses a finite life in which a song can leave its maker, change in other hands, and still be heard.
