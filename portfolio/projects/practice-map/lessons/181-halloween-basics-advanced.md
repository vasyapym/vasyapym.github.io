# Halloween: the basics to the advanced
A Beginner and Professional/Geek Friendly Guide in Prose

Halloween, written in older style as Hallowe'en, is a festival celebrated primarily on the evening of October 31st. In one sentence, it is a night about death, darkness, fear, disguise, and play, where a culture that normally avoids thinking about mortality deliberately invites it into the street, dresses as it, laughs at it, eats candy in the shape of it, and then sends it away again. That combination of genuinely ancient ideas about the dead, Christian religious observance, folk magic, immigration, childhood ritual, horror entertainment, and modern commerce is why Halloween feels both simple and strangely deep. A child understands it immediately. A historian, anthropologist, psychologist, or folklorist can study it for a lifetime.

The word itself explains much of the history. Hallow is an old English word for a holy person, a saint. Even, shortened to e'en, means evening. Halloween is All Hallows' Even, the evening before All Saints' Day on November 1st, which is followed by All Souls' Day on November 2nd. Together these three days, October 31st to November 2nd, are called Allhallowtide in older Christian tradition: a triduum, a three-day period, for remembering saints, martyrs, and all the ordinary dead. The modern secular Halloween has largely detached itself from church observance for most celebrants, but the calendar placement and the preoccupation with the dead are not accidental.

Beneath and beside the Christian festival lies Samhain, pronounced SOW-in, not sam-hain. Samhain was a major Celtic festival in Ireland, Scotland, and related cultures, marking November 1st as the beginning of winter and, in the pastoral calendar, a kind of new year. It was one of the four great quarter festivals alongside Imbolc in February, Beltane in May, and Lughnasadh in August. Harvest was in, cattle were brought down from summer pasture, animals that could not be fed through winter were slaughtered, bonfires were lit, and accounts were settled. In Irish literature and later folklore, Samhain was a time when the Aos Si, the people of the mounds, fairies or ancestral spirits in a pre-Christian cosmology, could move more freely into the human world.

Scholars at PhD level will immediately add a caution here that popular accounts often omit. The relationship between Samhain and Halloween is real but not a simple unbroken pagan survival. The great British historian Ronald Hutton, in The Stations of the Sun (1996), argued that we have surprisingly little direct evidence for exactly what pre-Christian Samhain rituals looked like, and that the Church may have placed All Saints on November 1st for Roman reasons independent of Ireland, with the festivals only later influencing each other in the British Isles. Other historians, like Lisa Morton and Nicholas Rogers, trace a syncretism, a blending, rather than a theft: Celtic ideas about a dangerous winter threshold, Roman festivals for the dead such as Parentalia and for the harvest goddess Pomona, whose symbol was the apple, and Christian prayer for souls in purgatory all layered onto the same darkening season in northern Europe. Halloween is not purely pagan and not purely Christian. It is folk culture, which rarely respects clean categories.

That seasonal threshold is the first advanced concept: liminality. Anthropologists Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner used liminal, from the Latin limen for threshold, to describe the middle stage of a rite where a person or a society is neither one thing nor the other: not child, not adult; not summer, not winter; not living, not dead. Halloween is culturally liminal. It happens at dusk, the threshold of day and night, at the threshold of autumn and winter, and folklore says the veil between worlds thins. Athin place in Celtic Christian spirituality is a location where heaven and earth feel close. On Halloween, time itself is imagined as thin. Masks help this idea, because the wearer is also liminal, neither fully themselves nor fully the monster they portray.

The second concept is apotropaic magic, from Greek apotrepein, to turn away. Much Halloween imagery that looks merely decorative was once protective. A carved face with a candle inside was meant to frighten harmful spirits, not attract them. Dressing as a ghost or demon, called guising in Scotland, could confuse or impersonate the wandering dead so they would leave you alone. Noise, bonfires, grotesque masks, and later fireworks, all turn away evil by being more frightening or brighter than it. The jack-o'-lantern is the perfect example. In Ireland and Scotland it was originally a turnip, swede, or mangel-wurzel, laboriously hollowed. Irish immigrants in 19th-century America discovered the native pumpkin was larger, softer, cheaper, and brilliantly orange, and the tradition transformed. The name comes from folklore about Stingy Jack, a trickster who bargained with the Devil, was barred from both heaven and hell, and wanders earth with a burning coal in a hollowed turnip. It also connects to ignis fatuus, foolish fire, the will-o'-the-wisp lights seen over marshes.

Door-to-door ritual is older than the phrase trick-or-treat. In medieval and early modern England, souling involved the poor, and later children, going door to door on Allhallowtide offering prayers for the household's dead in exchange for soul cakes, small spiced cakes marked with a cross. In Scotland and Ireland, guisers performed a song, poem, joke, or trick in costume for food or coins. Mumming involved troupe performances of folk plays, often involving death and revival. When Irish and Scottish immigrants brought these customs to North America, and when towns in the 1920s and 1930s wanted to replace destructive Halloween pranking and vandalism with organized, supervised activity for children, the modern bargain crystallized. The phrase trick-or-treat was in print by 1927 in Alberta, Canada. It spread in the United States in the 1930s, paused during World War II sugar rationing, and exploded in the new car-centred suburbs after the war. The trick is now almost entirely theatrical, a fossilised threat meaning: give us a performance reward or we will perform mischief.

Modern vocabulary separates into people, creatures, places, objects, and actions, but folklorists separate more precisely by ontology, by what kind of being something is supposed to be. A ghost, from Old English gast, breath or spirit, is the surviving soul or personality of a dead person. An apparition is specifically something seen, a visual manifestation, and does not prove a ghost exists. A phantom is insubstantial and illusory. A spectre, from Latin spectrum, an appearance or image, is usually a dreadful ghost. A wraith in Scots tradition is a ghostly double of a living person seen just before or shortly after their death. A poltergeist, German for noisy ghost, does not mainly appear but moves objects, bangs, and throws, and in parapsychology was controversially linked to troubled adolescents rather than the dead. A revenant, from French revenir, to return, is different again: it comes back in bodily form. Vampires and some zombies are revenants.

The classic monster taxonomy encodes different human fears. The witch, Old English wicce, is a living human who accesses forbidden knowledge or power, historically feared especially when society wanted to police women, healers, outsiders, and religious dissenters. In modern Wicca and neopaganism, witch is reclaimed as a religious identity, and Samhain is one of the eight sabbats on the Wheel of the Year. A warlock was originally an oath-breaker, not simply a male witch. A necromancer, Greek nekros corpse plus manteia divination, originally communicated with the dead to learn the future, later in fantasy commanded them. A vampire, popularised from Eastern European folklore and fixed by Bram Stoker's Dracula in 1897, is a corpse that drinks blood or life, combining fears of disease, contagion, aristocratic exploitation, sexuality, and improper burial. Bats joined vampires only after Spanish explorers found blood-drinking bats in Latin America and named them after the older legend. A werewolf is Old English wer, man, plus wolf, in Greek lycanthrope, and expresses fear of losing human reason to animal appetite, tied to the moon because monthly cycles and night activity made symbolic sense. A ghoul, from Arabic ghul, eats corpses in graveyards. A zombie comes from Haitian Vodou zonbi, a soul or body enslaved after death, a horror born of slavery's fear of being worked even beyond death. George Romero in Night of the Living Dead in 1968 called his monsters ghouls, but audiences renamed them zombies and created the modern flesh-eating, infection-spreading version. A skeleton is death without flesh, honest and universal. A scarecrow is a human double made to guard crops, frightening precisely because it looks almost alive in a field where no person should stand still that long.

The atmosphere words are also precise if you listen to them. Spooky is mild, playful fear. Eerie is quiet wrongness, a place too still or a sound almost familiar. Creepy crawls on skin and implies a slow, perhaps human threat watching. Scary causes an immediate jolt. Terrifying overwhelms. Macabre, associated with the medieval Danse Macabre in which Death dances equally with pope and peasant, dwells pleasantly or grimly on corpses and mortality. Gruesome dwells on wounds. Ghastly is deathly pale and shocking. Sinister originally meant left-handed in Latin and became ominous because left was culturally distrusted. These are not synonyms. Horror professionals calibrate them like musicians use dynamics.

Objects carry similar layers. A tombstone, gravestone, and headstone are used interchangeably now, but a tomb originally covered a grave, a headstone stood at the head. A coffin is six-sided and tapered to the human body, a casket in American funeral language is rectangular and more decorative, a euphemism borrowed from a jewel box. A cauldron is cooking pot, womb, potion vessel, and in Macbeth a machine for prophecy. A broomstick is a domestic tool transformed into a vehicle, a perfect symbol for women's household labour turned into secret freedom, which is why feminist readings take it seriously. A black cat is lucky in some British and Japanese traditions, unlucky in other European traditions where cats were believed to be witches familiars, spirit companions. A spider belongs because webs appear in abandoned houses and because autumn is spider season. Candy corn, created in the 1880s, was originally Chicken Feed, an agricultural candy for a rural nation coloured like harvest corn, now so divisive it measures nostalgic identity as much as flavour.

The games were once divination. Bobbing for apples, likely absorbing Roman Pomona symbolism and Celtic marriage fortune-telling, asked who would marry first. Peeling an apple in one long strip and throwing it over the shoulder would reveal a future spouse's initial. Burning nuts named for lovers, looking into a mirror at midnight while walking backwards down stairs, or setting a dumb supper, a silent meal with a place laid for the dead, were methods of scrying, seeing hidden knowledge in reflective or liminal conditions. Modern Bloody Mary mirror rituals in school bathrooms are direct descendants in children's folklore. A seance, French for a sitting, became popular with 19th-century Spiritualism after the Fox sisters in 1848, when grief after civil wars made speaking to the dead a parlour technology. The Ouija board, patented as a talking board in 1890, commercialised that hunger.

The deeper theories explain why this endures. Memento mori, Latin for remember you must die, is the medieval practice of keeping skulls, hourglasses, and rotting fruit in art not to be morbid but to make life urgent. Halloween is a popular, democratic memento mori. Sigmund Freud's 1919 essay on the uncanny, das Unheimliche, the unhomely, argued that fear strikes when something familiar becomes strange: a doll that might be alive, a double, a house that knows you. Haunted houses work because a home should be safest. Julia Kristeva's concept of abjection describes horror at what crosses the body's borders and reminds us we are meat: blood, corpses, decay. Mikhail Bakhtin's carnivalesque explains the laughter, inversion, costumes, and licensed misrule: for one night children command adults, beggars get gifts, the poor monster is king, death is mocked, and then normal order returns stronger. Ernest Becker in The Denial of Death (1973), and later Terror Management Theory in social psychology, argued that much culture is a shield against mortality awareness. Halloween both raises mortality salience, experiments show death reminders change behaviour, and allows mastery over it.

Modern recreational fear research adds a friendlier mechanism. Mathias Clasen and Coltan Scrivner describe horror as a simulation system: we calibrate fear in safety, learn our own limits, practise coping, and enjoy benign masochism, pleasurable sensations from initially negative signals when the brain knows there is no real danger, like chilli heat or a rollercoaster. A good haunted attraction, staffed by scareactors, is designed arousal management. Too little is boring, too much becomes panic or aggression. Extreme haunts that simulate kidnapping or torture remain ethically controversial because consent cannot fully solve the power problem when fear is real.

Debunking is also part of expert Halloween literacy. The poisoned candy panic is the central example. Sociologist Joel Best studied Halloween sadism claims for decades and found no evidence of strangers killing children with poisoned Halloween candy in the United States. The famous 1974 case was a father, Ronald O'Bryan, who poisoned his own son for insurance money and used trick-or-treating as cover. Razor blades in apples are overwhelmingly hoaxes or false reports. The danger is real cars, real visibility, real allergies, hence reflective costumes, inspected treats, and the modern Teal Pumpkin Project signalling non-food treats for allergic children. Likewise, Samhain was not a Celtic god of the dead, despite endless internet repetition. That error comes from 18th and 19th-century misreadings. The Devil in Christian theology, Halloween opposition in some churches, harvest festivals, and trunk-or-treat in church parking lots are modern negotiations, not ancient survivals. It is also historically sloppy to call Dia de los Muertos Mexican Halloween. It is a distinct Mexican tradition on November 1st and 2nd, with ofrendas, altars, photographs, food, marigolds called cempasuchil, and sugar skull calaveras, in which families welcome and feed beloved dead in a more intimate, less frightening register. Japan's Obon, China's Hungry Ghost Festival in the seventh lunar month, Wales's Nos Galan Gaeaf, the Isle of Man's Hop-tu-Naa, and Germany's Walpurgisnacht on April 30th are cousins in the human calendar of the dead, not copies.

Contemporary Halloween is also commerce and art. The National Retail Federation has counted American Halloween spending above ten billion dollars in recent years. Anoka, Minnesota, proclaimed itself Halloween Capital of the World after organising parades in 1920 to stop pranks. John Carpenter's film Halloween in 1978, with Michael Myers, cemented the modern slasher and made October horror releases an industry. Home haunts grew from decorated yards to engineering projects. Creepypasta, Slender Man, SCP Foundation stories, and backrooms lore are digital folklore: anonymous, copied, mutated stories following the same rules folklorists once tracked in oral ghost tales. Samhainophobia is an irrational fear of Halloween itself. The geek contemporary vocabulary includes cosplay, distinguished from costume by performance and identity craft, final girl in slasher theory, jump scare, found footage, folk horror, cosmic horror, body horror, and the monster theory of Jeffrey Jerome Cohen, whose first thesis was that the monster's body is a cultural body, a society writing its fears onto flesh.

For a beginner, therefore, Halloween is dressing up, saying trick-or-treat, carving a pumpkin, and being safely scared. For a professional, it is a calendar ritual at a seasonal hinge, a Christian-pagan-folk syncretism, an apotropaic masquerade, a children's redistribution custom descended from souling, a memento mori carnival, a laboratory for fear, and a multi-billion dollar performance of cultural anxieties about death, disease, strangers, sexuality, childhood, and what returns when we bury it. Those two accounts do not contradict each other. The child's plastic pumpkin is carrying, quite literally, two thousand years of prayer, hunger, winter, story, and rebellion against the dark.
