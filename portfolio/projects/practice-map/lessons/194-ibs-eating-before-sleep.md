# Late dinner, lying down


Vera proofreads cookery books. All day she queries other people's certainties — *source?* beside «sear to seal in the juices», *who says?* beside anything involving alkaline water — and at 22:15 she eats reheated dal standing at the counter, because that is when the day lets go of her. By 23:00 she is horizontal. By 00:40 something behind her navel is rearranging furniture.

Her grandmother had a sentence for this, delivered like weather: *Don't eat within three or four hours of bed; lying down, the food presses on a sensitive gut all night.*

Vera has irritable bowel syndrome with visceral hypersensitivity — a gut-and-brain circuit that reports ordinary stretch as discomfort — so the sentence is addressed to her. One night, awake anyway, she does to it what she does to sentences: takes it phrase by phrase, pencil in hand.

## «Lying down»

*Mark: stands, for a narrower reason than given.*

The gut does not need gravity to move food; it is a muscular tube that pushes. Astronauts digest. What gravity does help with is the light stuff. In a Barcelona laboratory, researchers ran gas into the small intestine of healthy volunteers and measured how quickly it came out the other end: faster upright than lying on the back. The same group had already shown that people with IBS, given a gas load, hold on to more of it and feel it more. Join the two — the joining is inference, not experiment — and a horizontal body is a slower exhaust for exactly the cargo an IBS gut tolerates worst.

Lying down also tips the stomach's contents toward its upper end, beside the junction with the oesophagus. That is part of the mechanics of night-time reflux, and reflux overlaps with IBS often enough to matter here. (The left side keeps the junction above the pool, which is why it is the kinder side to sleep on.)

## «Presses»

*Mark: query. Presses on what, from where?*

The nerve endings behind visceral pain sit in the gut wall and report stretch and tension — pressure from the inside. That is how hypersensitivity is measured: a small balloon is inflated in the rectum, and a large share of people with IBS, though not all, report discomfort at volumes healthy volunteers barely notice. So «presses» is the right verb with the wrong picture. Nothing lies on the gut like a paperweight. What stretches it at night is a full stomach, gas, and the gut's own contractions. That the mattress, or the body's weight on its side, adds anything has, to my knowledge, never been measured.

A meal also does more than fill the stomach; within minutes it tells the colon to get moving. This gastrocolic response is larger after bigger and fattier meals, and in IBS it tends to be stronger, or more strongly felt. Fat goes further: in experiments, fat delivered into the upper small intestine made the lower bowel more sensitive to the balloon, and more so in IBS than in controls. A late creamy dinner turns up the signal and the gain at once.

## «All night»

*Mark: delete.*

The standard test of stomach emptying uses a small low-fat meal and calls the result normal if no more than a tenth remains at four hours. A real dinner, larger and fattier, takes longer, and small studies suggest solids empty more slowly in the evening than in the morning. Still: hours, not all night.

And the night itself is the gut's quietest shift. The colon goes nearly silent in sleep and wakes when you do; its big propulsive contractions cluster after waking and after meals. Once the small intestine is empty of nutrients it starts its fasting programme, the migrating motor complex — a band of contractions that sweeps from the stomach to the end of the small intestine roughly every hour and a half to two hours, clearing leftovers. Eating switches it off; a late dinner postpones the first sweep. Whether that postponement harms anyone is plausible, popular and unproven.

Two clinical observations agree. Bloating in IBS typically builds through the day and is lowest on waking. And symptoms that regularly wake a person — night-time diarrhoea above all — are treated by clinicians as a reason to look beyond IBS, not as its signature.

So the trouble, when there is trouble, lives in a window: the first hours after the meal, which a late dinner happens to place in bed. The timing is unkind in three ways. Stomach stretch and the colon's response are at their peak. The fermentable part of the meal starts reaching the colon a few hours after eating, so its gas arrives after lights out, into the slow exhaust. And a person lying still in the dark has nothing else to listen to.

## «Sensitive gut»

*Mark: stands — and points somewhere the sentence didn't.*

The link with the most direct IBS evidence is not mechanical at all. It is sleep. In a diary study of women with IBS, a poor night predicted more abdominal pain the next day, while a bad day did not reliably predict a bad night. Sleep loss lowers pain thresholds in general. A dinner that costs Vera ninety minutes of sleep can bill her tomorrow, no lasting mechanical insult required.

## «Three or four hours»

*Mark: query. Source?*

The number has two respectable parents, neither of them an IBS trial. Reflux guidelines suggest two to three hours between the last meal and bed, on modest evidence. And four hours is the textbook horizon of stomach emptying. For IBS itself I know of no randomised trial of a fixed pre-sleep gap; what exists is observational and tangled up with shift work, stress and what late meals tend to consist of. First-line dietary advice for IBS asks for regular meals, eaten unhurriedly, without long gaps — and that last clause cuts the other way: a hungry night is not obviously better than a small plain snack.

## The sentence, corrected

Vera writes it out clean:

*If your gut is sensitive, the loud part of a meal lasts a few hours — longer if the meal is large, fatty or fermentable. Try to have it over before you lie still in the dark. Three hours is a fair first guess, not a finding; what and how much probably matter more than when.*

Verdict: a reasonable personal experiment, resting on real mechanisms and no direct proof. The paperweight was never there.

She moves dinner to 20:00 for a fortnight, keeps the dal, halves the portion, and notes each morning how the night went. The furniture still shifts now and then, but no longer at 00:40. Which change did it, the clock or the half plate, her notes cannot say; she changed both at once, which she would have queried in anyone else's manuscript. In the margin of her grandmother's sentence she pencils *stet* — proofreader's Latin for «let it stand» — and then, being who she is, adds: *as amended.*
