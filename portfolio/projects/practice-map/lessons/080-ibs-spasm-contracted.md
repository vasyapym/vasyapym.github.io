# IBS Spasm: What the Contraction Really Is

## 0. The whole model in one paragraph

Irritable bowel syndrome is not a broken gut; it is a mis-tuned control system. The hardware — mucosa, muscle, nerves — passes every structural test, but the firmware runs with the sensor sensitivity too high, the amplifier gain too high, the noise-cancelling switched off, and a motor that fires in jittery, poorly coordinated bursts. This is why the Rome IV criteria reclassified it as a "disorder of gut–brain interaction." Every lifehack that actually works pulls one of four levers: reduce the input (what stretches, irritates or ferments in the lumen), lower peripheral gain (calm the muscle and nerve endings in the bowel wall), lower central gain (retune the spinal cord and brain), or restore descending inhibition (the brain's built-in analgesic system, which runs on vagal tone, sleep and a sense of safety). Hold that map; everything below is a location on it.

## 1. What a "spasm" physically is

The colon is driven by a pacemaker network, the interstitial cells of Cajal, which generate rhythmic electrical slow waves; the enteric nervous system — roughly 500 million neurons, more than the spinal cord — decides which of those waves become actual contractions. Two contraction types matter. Segmenting, non-propagating contractions mix content and, when overactive, behave like a row of closed doors trapping pockets of gas and stool between them: the colicky cramp typical of constipation-predominant IBS. High-amplitude propagating contractions (HAPCs) are the mass movements that sweep the colon about six times a day in health; in diarrhea-predominant IBS they are more frequent and more often painful, and they are what you feel as urgency plus cramp shortly after eating. The IBS "spasm," then, is less a single muscle knotting in tetany and more a coordination failure — too much wall tension at the wrong moment, applied to a lumen already distended by gas or water. Distension is the key word. You can biopsy a colon without anesthesia; it barely registers cutting or burning. But it is exquisitely sensitive to stretch.

## 2. Why it hurts more than it should

Healthy people generate the same motor events and barely notice. IBS patients notice because of visceral hypersensitivity, which is measurable: inflate a balloon in the rectum and about 60% of patients report pain at volumes controls find trivial. The gain is turned up at three stages.

Peripherally, the nerve endings in the wall are sensitized. Mast cells cluster next to enteric nerves, their density correlates with pain severity, and they leak histamine and tryptase, which via H1 and protease-activated receptor 2 lower the firing threshold of afferents — a picture especially clear in the ~10% of cases that begin after an episode of infectious gastroenteritis. TRPV1, the capsaicin-and-heat receptor, is expressed about 3.5-fold higher in IBS rectal biopsies. Enterochromaffin cells store 90–95% of the body's serotonin and dump it on mechanical stretch; 5-HT3 receptors on afferents carry the pain-and-urgency signal, 5-HT4 receptors on enteric neurons drive propulsion, and the serotonin transporter that should mop up the signal is under-expressed in IBS mucosa.

Spinally, the dorsal horn undergoes central sensitization — the same NMDA-dependent "wind-up" seen in chronic somatic pain — so a given afferent volley produces a larger output. Visceral afferents also converge onto the same dorsal-horn neurons as skin and muscle afferents (viscerosomatic convergence), which is why the pain is diffuse and referred and, usefully, why skin-level input can compete with it.

Supraspinally, the brain's descending pain-inhibitory system — periaqueductal gray to rostral ventromedial medulla to dorsal horn — is measurably weaker; conditioned-pain-modulation testing shows IBS patients simply don't turn the volume down the way controls do. On top of that sits the stress axis: corticotropin-releasing factor released centrally under threat acts on CRF1 receptors to accelerate colonic motility and increase visceral sensitivity in a single stroke, which is the mechanistic reason an exam or an argument produces a cramp within minutes. Pain neuroscience education is therefore therapy, not consolation: catastrophizing drives anterior cingulate and amygdala outputs that suppress descending inhibition, so genuinely understanding that the pain is a mis-tuned alarm rather than tissue damage is a real gain reduction.

## 3. The inputs: what actually triggers a spasm

*The meal.* Food in the stomach fires the gastrocolic reflex — a neuro-hormonal surge (vagal, enteric, CCK, gastrin, serotonin) in colonic motility that begins within minutes. In IBS it is exaggerated, and it scales with caloric load and especially fat, which releases CCK and independently lowers colonic pain thresholds. Hence cramps clustering 15–60 minutes after eating, and hence the meal that hurts being the large, fatty, hurried one eaten after skipping lunch.

*Fermentable substrate.* FODMAPs — fructose, lactose, fructans, galacto-oligosaccharides, polyols — are small, poorly absorbed molecules. MRI studies show fructose osmotically pulling water into the small bowel and fructans generating hydrogen and methane in the colon. Water plus gas equals distension; distension in a sensitized colon equals pain. Everyone eats the onions and makes the gas; only some people feel it.

*Stimulants and irritants.* Coffee provokes distal colonic motor activity within four minutes at an intensity comparable to a 1000-kcal meal, and decaf retains roughly three-quarters of the effect, so it is not only the caffeine. Capsaicin activates precisely the TRPV1 receptors that are over-expressed. Alcohol raises permeability and motility. Sorbitol in gum and mints is a polyol; carbonation and gulped air add gas directly.

*State.* Stress via CRF, as above. Sleep loss lowers descending inhibition the next morning. Menstruation: uterine prostaglandins sensitize the rectum, barostat thresholds fall perimenstrually, and cycles track symptoms in many women.

## 4. Acute hacks: the ten-minute protocol, with the physics

*Heat.* A heat pad on the abdomen is not folklore. Warm-skin afferents from the same dermatome as the colon compete with visceral input at the convergent dorsal-horn neurons (gate control), and skin heating above ~40 °C has been proposed to switch off P2X3 receptors, the ATP-gated channels that transduce distension pain in hollow organs. Heat does not "relax the colon" from outside — it doesn't get that deep — it changes the spinal arithmetic.

*Slow diaphragmatic breathing.* About six breaths per minute with a longer exhale than inhale maximizes respiratory sinus arrhythmia, raises vagal tone, and dials down sympathetic and CRF drive; it is the fastest drug-free way to lean on descending inhibition. The mechanical side is a bonus: diaphragmatic excursion massages the colon and shifts trapped gas. Lying on the left side with knees drawn up, or walking slowly, exploits gravity and gentle motion toward the same end.

*Peppermint oil.* L-menthol blocks L-type voltage-gated calcium channels in gut smooth muscle — the same channel class cardiac calcium-channel blockers hit, but applied topically from the lumen — so the muscle cannot sustain the contraction. It also agonizes TRPM8, the cold receptor, which on visceral afferents dampens TRPV1 and TRPA1 signalling: a coolness the colon can't consciously feel but responds to. Enteric coating matters; released in the stomach, menthol relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter and gives you reflux instead of relief. Meta-analyses put the number-needed-to-treat near three, better than most prescription options.

*An antispasmodic in the pocket.* Hyoscine butylbromide is a quaternary amine that barely enters the bloodstream and blocks M3 muscarinic receptors on colonic muscle; dicyclomine adds direct smooth-muscle action; mebeverine, otilonium and pinaverium are "musculotropic," acting on sodium or calcium channels in the muscle without anticholinergic side effects. Pooled NNT is about five. The hack is timing: because the gastrocolic reflex is predictable, dosing thirty minutes before a meal you know is risky pre-empts the spasm rather than chasing it.

*Don't fight the toilet.* If the cramp is a propagating contraction, it is trying to empty the colon; go, and put your feet on a stool. Squatting relaxes puborectalis and straightens the anorectal angle, so evacuation needs less straining, and less straining means less rectal afferent bombardment.

*Don't panic.* This is a pharmacological instruction, not a platitude. The amygdala–CRF–sympathetic cascade is the one loop you can interrupt in real time, and it is the one that converts a cramp into an episode.

## 5. Chronic hacks: retuning the system

*Meal architecture.* Smaller, evenly spaced meals flatten the gastrocolic peaks; lower fat shrinks them further. Spacing also serves the migrating motor complex, the fasting-state "housekeeper" wave that sweeps the small bowel every 90–120 minutes and is abolished by any caloric intake — constant grazing means it plausibly never runs, leaving more substrate to ferment. Eat slowly: chewing reduces swallowed air and gives satiety signalling time to work. Keep timing regular; colonic motility is circadian, peaking on waking, so a fixed breakfast followed by an unhurried toilet visit stacks the gastrocolic reflex on top of the circadian surge.

*Low-FODMAP, done properly.* Two to six weeks of restriction helps roughly half to three-quarters of patients, mostly in bloating and pain. But it is a diagnostic tool, not a diet: the payload is the structured reintroduction, one food group at a time, to find your personal thresholds — essentially a bisection search over your inputs. Indefinite restriction starves Bifidobacteria, narrows the microbiome, and in susceptible people slides into disordered eating. Relax the constraint as far as tolerance allows. If you keep a symptom log, treat it as noisy time-series data with lag, not as a list of villains; single-observation causal claims are how people end up eating six foods.

*Fiber, but the right kind.* Psyllium is soluble, gel-forming and minimally fermentable: it normalizes stool water in both directions without generating gas. Wheat bran is insoluble and coarse; in the head-to-head trial it made symptoms worse. Titrate psyllium up slowly.

*Movement and sleep.* Moderate exercise lowers symptom scores in randomized trials, plausibly via transit, vagal tone and central pain modulation. Sleep debt is a next-day hypersensitivity multiplier; protect it as you would a medication.

*Bloating that looks physically impossible.* If your abdomen visibly distends within hours without a commensurate volume of gas, the culprit is often abdomino-phrenic dyssynergia: the diaphragm descends and the abdominal wall relaxes — the reverse of the correct reflex — pushing contents forward. Biofeedback retraining of the diaphragm corrects it; daily diaphragmatic-breathing practice is the home version.

## 6. The pharmacology map, in prose

Beyond antispasmodics and peppermint, the drugs sort by lever. Low-dose tricyclics (amitriptyline 10–30 mg, or nortriptyline) are the central-gain reducers: at these doses they are neuromodulators rather than antidepressants, acting on descending pathways and, through anticholinergic slowing, helpfully on diarrhea; the 2023 ATLANTIS trial confirmed benefit in ordinary primary-care patients. SSRIs do less for pain but suit constipation. On the serotonin axis, 5-HT3 antagonists (ondansetron, alosetron) quiet the afferent and slow transit for IBS-D; 5-HT4 agonists (prucalopride) accelerate transit for IBS-C. Linaclotide and plecanatide activate guanylate cyclase-C on the epithelium; the resulting cGMP pulls water into the lumen and, elegantly, leaks out the basolateral side to reduce afferent firing — an analgesic delivered from inside the pipe. Rifaximin, a non-absorbed antibiotic, improves bloating and global symptoms in IBS-D with an NNT of about ten, consistent with a fermentation-and-microbiome contribution. Probiotic evidence is heterogeneous; specific strains such as *Bifidobacterium infantis* 35624 have positive trials, but guidelines rightly refuse to generalize across the category. Loperamide fixes urgency but not pain; eluxadoline, a mixed µ-agonist/δ-antagonist, does somewhat better on composite endpoints but carries a pancreatitis risk in people without a gallbladder. H1 antihistamines (ebastine, ketotifen) have reduced hypersensitivity in trials and mark the mast-cell frontier.

## 7. The brain layer, which is not the soft option

Gut-directed hypnotherapy — the Manchester protocol, twelve sessions of imagery aimed specifically at gut control — produces response rates around 70% that persist for years, is non-inferior in group format, and now exists in app form. IBS-specific cognitive behavioural therapy performs comparably. These are not "coping": imaging shows them shifting anterior cingulate and insula activity and restoring descending inhibition. The same physiology explains why placebo response in IBS trials runs near 40% and why open-label placebo — pills the patient knows are inert — still outperformed no treatment in a randomized trial: expectation is a direct input to the descending inhibitory system. For a patient whose amplifier is the problem, these are the most targeted interventions on the list, not the last resort.

## 8. Imposters and red flags

Before optimizing, confirm you are optimizing the right system. Bile acid diarrhea — excess bile acids reaching the colon, where they drive secretion and HAPCs via TGR5 — masquerades as IBS-D in roughly a quarter to a third of cases and responds to sequestrants like colestyramine; a SeHCAT scan or a therapeutic trial finds it. Celiac serology and fecal calprotectin should be normal before the label sticks. Blood in the stool, weight loss, symptoms that wake you at night, anemia, onset after fifty, or a family history of bowel cancer or IBD are not IBS features and need a colonoscope, not a lifehack. And all of the above is education, not a prescription: dosing decisions belong with a clinician who knows your history.

## 9. Synthesis

Model yourself as a signal chain. The lumen is the input: control its volume, fat, fermentability and timing. The bowel wall is the first amplifier: peppermint, antispasmodics and pre-emptive dosing cap its gain. The cord and brain are the second amplifier: neuromodulators, hypnotherapy, education and sleep turn it down. Descending inhibition is your noise-cancelling: breathing, exercise and non-panic keep it powered. Acute episodes are handled at the first two stages in minutes; the condition is handled at the last two over months. Nothing in this system is broken — but the gain settings are yours to change.
