# Italian Neorealism: A Lesson

## Why This Movement Matters

Most film movements are remembered for a look: German Expressionism's crooked sets, film noir's venetian-blind shadows, the French New Wave's jump cuts. Italian Neorealism is different. It is remembered for an *ethic*. It is a set of ideas about what cinema owes to reality, and those ideas still shape how filmmakers think about truth on screen.

Between roughly 1943 and 1952, a small group of Italian filmmakers, working in a country wrecked by war, made films about ordinary people: unemployed workers, street kids, pensioners, partisans, fishermen. They often shot in real streets, cast non-actors, and refused tidy endings. The result reorganized global film culture. Satyajit Ray, the French New Wave, Iranian cinema of the 1990s, the Dardenne brothers, and recent American indie realism all descend from it.

This lesson builds the subject in layers: historical context, core concepts, key films, the theory behind them, the myths that need correcting, and the legacy.

---

## Part One: The Historical Setup

To understand Neorealism, you first need to know what it was reacting against.

**Fascist-era cinema.** Mussolini took cinema seriously. He reportedly called it "the strongest weapon." The regime founded the Centro Sperimentale di Cinematografia, a national film school, in 1935 and opened the massive Cinecittà studio complex in Rome in 1937. These institutions made Italy's film industry technically sophisticated. Much of the popular output, though, was escapist: polished bourgeois comedies and melodramas later nicknamed *telefoni bianchi*, or "white telephone films," after the luxurious white telephones that signaled wealth on screen. These films showed an Italy of glamorous apartments and romantic misunderstandings, a fantasy world sealed off from poverty, politics, and dissent.

**An irony worth noting.** The fascist infrastructure also trained the people who would dismantle its aesthetic. Many future Neorealists studied at the Centro Sperimentale or wrote for the film journals *Cinema* (edited, remarkably, by Mussolini's son Vittorio) and *Bianco e Nero*. In these pages, young critics like Giuseppe De Santis and Michelangelo Antonioni argued for a cinema rooted in Italian landscapes, Italian literature, and Italian lives. The intellectual groundwork was laid *inside* the system before the system collapsed.

**The collapse.** In 1943 the Allies invaded Sicily, Mussolini was deposed, and Germany occupied northern and central Italy. A brutal period of occupation, resistance, and civil war followed until 1945. When it ended, Italy was physically and morally shattered. Cinecittà had been damaged and was partly used as a displaced persons camp. Cities were in ruins, and unemployment and black markets were everywhere.

This crisis created both a *practical* condition and a *moral* one. Practically, filmmakers had limited access to studios, money, and resources, which pushed them into the streets. Morally, after twenty years of official lies, many artists felt an obligation to show the country as it actually was. Neorealism grew out of both.

## Part Two: Core Concepts

There was never a single Neorealist manifesto signed by everyone. It is better to think of Neorealism as a *cluster of tendencies*. No film has all of them, but the major works share most. Here are the key ones.

### 1. Ordinary People as Protagonists
The heroes are not aristocrats, detectives, or stars playing glamorous types. They are workers, children, the elderly, the poor. The drama comes from economic and social pressure rather than from villains or grand destinies. The question driving a Neorealist plot is often brutally simple: *Will this man find work? Will this old man make rent?*

### 2. Location Shooting
Films were shot in real streets, apartments, villages, and ruins. The city is not a backdrop. It is evidence. When you see bombed-out Berlin in Rossellini's *Germany Year Zero* (1948), you are not looking at a set designer's idea of devastation. You are looking at the devastation itself.

### 3. Non-Professional Actors (Often Mixed with Professionals)
Directors frequently cast people from the very social worlds the films depicted. The lead in *Bicycle Thieves* (1948), Lamberto Maggiorani, was a factory worker. The title role in *Umberto D.* (1952) went to Carlo Battisti, a university professor of linguistics. The idea was that a real face carries a lived history that performance can't fully fake.

### 4. Dedramatization and the Rejection of Classical Plot
Hollywood's classical style, the dominant model then and now, is built on cause and effect. A goal-driven hero faces escalating obstacles toward a climax and a resolution. Every scene "earns its keep" by advancing the plot.

Neorealism loosens this machine. Scenes linger on moments that do not advance the story: a maid grinding coffee, a man waiting, a boy wandering. Coincidence and contingency matter. Endings are often unresolved or bleak. Life doesn't climax; it continues.

### 5. *Pedinamento*: "Shadowing"
This concept comes from **Cesare Zavattini**, the screenwriter behind De Sica's major films and the movement's most important theorist. *Pedinamento* literally means "tailing" or "shadowing," like a detective following a suspect. Zavattini wanted the camera to follow a person through their day with patient attention, discovering the drama hidden inside ordinary time. He famously imagined that a film could take a trivial event, such as a woman buying a pair of shoes, and reveal its full human and social weight through sustained observation.

### 6. Social Critique Without (Usually) Sermonizing
These films are political. They expose unemployment, poverty, bureaucratic indifference, and the failures of postwar society. But at their best they show rather than lecture. The audience is trusted to draw conclusions.

### 7. Choral and Episodic Structure
Some Neorealist films spread attention across communities rather than concentrating on a single hero. Rossellini's *Paisà* (1946) is built from six separate episodes following the Allied advance up the Italian peninsula, with no continuing protagonist. The *nation* is the subject.

**A useful way to hold all this together:** classical cinema asks, *What happens next?* Neorealism asks, *What is it like to be here, now, in this situation?*

## Part Three: The Key Figures and Films

### The Prologue: Luchino Visconti, *Ossessione* (1943)
Made under fascism, *Ossessione* was an unauthorized adaptation of James M. Cain's *The Postman Always Rings Twice*, transplanted to the dusty roads and trattorias of the Po Valley. Its sweaty, sordid, provincial atmosphere was so far from white-telephone gloss that fascist authorities found it scandalous. The term *neorealismo* began circulating in connection with it. Visconti, an aristocrat and Marxist, had worked as an assistant to Jean Renoir in France, and he absorbed the influence of French Poetic Realism.

### Roberto Rossellini: The War Trilogy
**Rome, Open City** (*Roma città aperta*, 1945) is the film that announced Neorealism to the world. Shot shortly after Rome's liberation, it depicts the Nazi occupation and the Resistance, centered on a priest (Aldo Fabrizi) and a working-class woman, Pina (Anna Magnani). Its co-writers included a young Federico Fellini. Pina's death, running after a truck carrying her arrested fiancé, is one of the most famous shots in cinema history. The film has a raw, almost newsreel urgency, even though, as we'll see, it is more constructed than legend suggests.

**Paisà** (1946) is the episodic journey from Sicily to the Po Delta. The final episode, among partisans in the marshes, is often cited as the movement's purest achievement in stark, unsentimental observation.

**Germany Year Zero** (1948) follows a boy, Edmund, through the ruins of Berlin. It is a devastating study of moral collapse, and its ending remains one of the bleakest in film.

Rossellini's style is the most *documentary-like* and morally intense of the Neorealists. He later moved toward more spiritual and modernist work, especially with Ingrid Bergman in *Stromboli* (1950), *Europe '51* (1952), and *Journey to Italy* (1954).

### Vittorio De Sica and Cesare Zavattini: The Partnership
De Sica was a popular matinee idol turned director. Paired with Zavattini, he produced the movement's most emotionally powerful works.

**Shoeshine** (*Sciuscià*, 1946) follows two boys who shine shoes for American soldiers and end up in a juvenile prison. It received an honorary Academy Award, a precursor to the Best Foreign Language Film category.

**Bicycle Thieves** (*Ladri di biciclette*, 1948) is the canonical Neorealist film, the one to watch first. Antonio, an unemployed man in Rome, finally gets a job pasting posters, but it requires a bicycle. On his first day, the bicycle is stolen. He and his young son Bruno search the city for it. That's essentially the whole plot. From this tiny premise the film builds a portrait of a society where one stolen object can destroy a family, and where the poor are pitted against the poor. It is often cited as one of the greatest films ever made. (A widely repeated anecdote holds that a Hollywood producer offered financing if De Sica would cast Cary Grant. Whether or not it happened exactly that way, it captures the stakes: a star would have destroyed the film's premise.)

**Umberto D.** (1952) portrays an elderly retired civil servant struggling to survive on a pension, accompanied only by his dog. It contains the most famous example of Zavattini's ideas in practice: a long sequence in which a young maid wakes up and goes through her morning routine in the kitchen. Nothing "happens," and yet the scene became a touchstone for critics arguing that cinema could find meaning in pure duration.

### Visconti Again: *La terra trema* (1948)
Loosely adapted from Giovanni Verga's novel *I Malavoglia*, this epic was shot in the Sicilian fishing village of Aci Trezza with local fishermen as actors, speaking Sicilian dialect so dense that the film needed an Italian voice-over narration for mainland audiences. It tells the story of a family's failed attempt to escape exploitation by wholesalers. It is formally composed, almost operatic in its visual grandeur, which shows that "realism" did not mean "artless."

### Others Worth Knowing
- **Giuseppe De Santis**, *Bitter Rice* (*Riso amaro*, 1949): rice-field workers, melodrama, and sex appeal (Silvana Mangano). It shows how Neorealism could hybridize with popular genres.
- **Alberto Lattuada** and **Pietro Germi** extended the approach into crime and social drama.

## Part Four: The Theory Layer

This is where the movement becomes intellectually rich. Neorealism mattered not only because of the films, but because critics and theorists used it to rethink what cinema *is*.

### Zavattini: The Practitioner-Theorist
In his essay usually translated as "Some Ideas on the Cinema" (1952–53), Zavattini argued that cinema's true task is to confront reality directly, without the mediation of invented stories. The ideal is to make the everyday visible: to "analyze" a fact until its full significance appears. In his most radical formulation, the filmmaker shouldn't invent plots at all, because reality already contains infinite stories. This position is idealistic and arguably impossible in pure form, but it pushed cinema in a direction that remains productive.

### André Bazin: The Philosopher of Realism
The French critic **André Bazin**, co-founder of *Cahiers du cinéma*, became Neorealism's most influential champion. His ideas rest on a claim from his essay "The Ontology of the Photographic Image": the photograph is not just a picture *of* something. It is causally produced by the light of the thing itself, a kind of physical trace, like a fingerprint or a death mask. Cinema, therefore, has a unique relationship to reality. It doesn't merely represent the world; it *records* it.

From this premise, Bazin valued styles that respect the integrity of reality:
- **Long takes** that preserve the continuity of time,
- **Deep focus** that lets viewers explore the frame,
- **Restraint** in editing and manipulation.

He contrasted this with montage-heavy styles, like Soviet cinema, that construct meaning by cutting reality into pieces and reassembling it.

Two of his Neorealism-specific ideas matter most:

**The "fact-image."** Writing about *Paisà*, Bazin argued that Rossellini's scenes are built from blocks of reality that keep their ambiguity. Classical editing breaks events into pre-digested units, each with one meaning. Rossellini instead gives us fragments with the density and openness of real events, and the viewer must bridge the gaps between them. The ellipses in *Paisà* are not like jumps in a staircase. They are like stepping stones across a river: you have to make the leap yourself.

**"No more cinema."** Writing on *Bicycle Thieves*, Bazin offered a famous paradox: no more actors, no more story, no more sets. In the perfect aesthetic illusion of reality, *there is no more cinema*. He meant that the film's artistry is so complete it becomes invisible. This is not artlessness but art that effaces itself.

### Gilles Deleuze: The Philosophical Afterlife
Decades later, in *Cinema 2: The Time-Image* (1985), French philosopher **Gilles Deleuze** placed Neorealism at the hinge of cinema history.

His argument runs like this. Classical cinema is organized around the **"movement-image"**: characters perceive a situation, react, and act on it, in a sensory-motor chain linking perception to action. After World War II, Deleuze argues, that chain broke. In Neorealism, characters increasingly find themselves in situations they cannot act upon. They become *seers* rather than *agents*. They witness, wander, and absorb.

Deleuze calls these **"pure optical and sound situations."** Think of Ingrid Bergman in *Stromboli*, overwhelmed by the brutal tuna fishing and the volcano, or the maid in *Umberto D.* simply existing in her kitchen. When action no longer organizes the image, *time itself* becomes visible. This is the birth of the **"time-image,"** which Deleuze traces forward into Antonioni, Fellini, the New Wave, and modernist cinema generally.

**Geek-friendly translation:** If classical cinema runs on an input → process → output loop (see threat → decide → act), Neorealism is what happens when the output stage stalls. The character keeps receiving input but cannot convert it into effective action. Deleuze argues this "stall" opened a whole new mode of cinema.

## Part Five: Myths and Complications

A PhD-level understanding requires knowing where the textbook story is too simple.

**Myth 1: Neorealist films were shot entirely on location.**
Not quite. *Rome, Open City* used constructed interiors for some scenes. Studio work never disappeared. Location shooting was a *tendency* and a selling point, not an absolute rule.

**Myth 2: They used only non-professional actors.**
False for many key films. *Rome, Open City*'s leads, Anna Magnani and Aldo Fabrizi, were established performers. Mixing professionals with non-professionals was common, and the friction between the two registers is part of the aesthetic.

**Myth 3: What you hear is direct, raw reality.**
Italian cinema routinely post-synchronized dialogue, meaning it was recorded or re-recorded in a studio after filming. This practice actually *enabled* Neorealism. You can shoot in a noisy street, and you can coach non-actors, if the sound is added later. Some non-professionals were even voiced by others. The "real" sound is often manufactured.

**Myth 4: Neorealism was artless, a camera simply pointed at life.**
These films are carefully constructed. *Bicycle Thieves* is tightly structured, with deliberate symbolic patterning and an emotionally calibrated score. *La terra trema* is composed like a painting. Neorealism is a *style of realism*, not the absence of style. As a rule of thumb, "realism" in art is always an effect produced by technique.

**Myth 5: Neorealism was popular with Italian audiences.**
Generally, no. Many Neorealist films did better abroad, among critics and international art-house audiences, than at home, where Italians often preferred Hollywood films and domestic comedies. A war-weary public wasn't always eager to pay to see its own misery.

**Myth 6: It was a unified movement.**
It was more a loose convergence of directors with different politics and temperaments. Rossellini was drawn toward Catholic humanism and spiritual questions, Visconti toward Marxism and operatic form, De Sica and Zavattini toward a humanist realism with strong emotional pull. Arguments about who was "really" Neorealist began almost immediately.

## Part Six: The Decline (c. 1949–1955)

Several pressures ended the movement's core phase.

**Political pressure.** In 1948 the Christian Democrats defeated the left in a pivotal election, and Cold War politics hardened. Giulio Andreotti, the government official overseeing cinema, championed the 1949 legislation (often called the "Andreotti law") that tied state support and export licenses to films' content. He is associated with the phrase *"i panni sporchi si lavano in famiglia"*: "dirty laundry should be washed at home." In other words, stop showing the world Italy's poverty. He publicly criticized *Umberto D.* for giving a damaging image of the nation.

**Economic change.** As Italy recovered and approached its "economic miracle," the desperate conditions that fueled Neorealism receded, and Hollywood flooded the Italian market.

**Commercial dilution.** "Pink Neorealism" (*neorealismo rosa*) emerged: films that kept the working-class settings and picturesque locations but swapped critique for sentimental comedy, as in *Bread, Love and Dreams* (1953).

**Artistic evolution.** The directors themselves moved on. Rossellini's *Journey to Italy* (1954) turned inward toward existential, spiritual drama. Fellini's *La Strada* (1954) sparked fierce debate, with Marxist critics accusing it of betraying Neorealism's social commitment in favor of private, poetic mysticism. These debates mark the shift from Neorealism to European art-cinema modernism, which is arguably Neorealism's own child.

## Part Seven: Legacy

Neorealism's influence is hard to overstate, because it offered something rare: a *portable* model. You didn't need Hollywood budgets. You needed a camera, real places, real people, and something urgent to say. That made it adaptable worldwide.

- **India:** Satyajit Ray saw *Bicycle Thieves* in London in 1950 and resolved to make *Pather Panchali* (1955) in a similar spirit, with location shooting and non-professional performers.
- **France:** The New Wave inherited the street shooting, the loosened narrative, and Bazin's critical framework. Bazin mentored Truffaut.
- **Latin America:** Brazil's Cinema Novo and the broader "Third Cinema" movement adapted Neorealist methods to anti-colonial politics. Several key Latin American filmmakers studied at the Centro Sperimentale in Rome.
- **Iran:** Abbas Kiarostami, Jafar Panahi, and others blended non-professional actors, child protagonists, and documentary-fiction ambiguity in ways often compared to Neorealism.
- **Contemporary realism:** Ken Loach, the Dardenne brothers, Kelly Reichardt (*Wendy and Lucy* is practically a *Umberto D.* descendant), Sean Baker (*The Florida Project*), Chloé Zhao (*Nomadland*), and Alfonso Cuarón (*Roma*) all work in traditions traceable to it.

Even outside art cinema, the vocabulary persists. Whenever a film earns credibility through real locations, unknown faces, or attention to everyday labor and economic precarity, it is drawing on Neorealism's account.

## Viewing Guide: Where to Start

Watch in this order for a smooth learning curve:

1. **_Bicycle Thieves_ (De Sica, 1948).** The essential entry point. Emotionally direct and structurally clear.
2. **_Rome, Open City_ (Rossellini, 1945).** Historical urgency and the movement's founding shock.
3. **_Umberto D._ (De Sica, 1952).** Zavattini's ideas at their purest. Watch the maid's morning sequence closely.
4. **_Paisà_ (Rossellini, 1946).** The episodic structure and Bazin's "fact-image" in action.
5. **_Germany Year Zero_ (Rossellini, 1948).** Neorealism exported to the ruins of the enemy.
6. **_La terra trema_ (Visconti, 1948).** For when you're ready for a long, demanding, formally grand film.
7. **_Ossessione_ (Visconti, 1943).** The proto-Neorealist prologue, especially rewarding if you know noir.

**While watching, ask:**
- Which scenes do *not* advance the plot? What do they give you instead?
- Can you spot the non-professionals versus the trained actors? Does the mix feel seamless or productively jarring?
- How much does the city or landscape "speak"?
- Where does the film's realism feel constructed: music, framing, symbolic patterns?
- Does the protagonist *act* on their world, or mostly *endure* and *witness* it? (Deleuze's question.)

## Quick Glossary

- **Telefoni bianchi:** Glossy, escapist fascist-era comedies and melodramas.
- **Pedinamento:** Zavattini's "shadowing" method, following ordinary lives with patient attention.
- **Dedramatization:** Reducing plot-driven tension in favor of everyday duration and observation.
- **Post-synchronization:** Recording dialogue after filming. Common in Italy, and crucial to Neorealist practice.
- **Fact-image:** Bazin's term for Rossellini's scene-units that keep the ambiguity of real events.
- **Time-image:** Deleuze's concept for cinema in which time, not action, organizes the image.
- **Pink Neorealism:** Later sentimental-comic films that kept Neorealist settings but abandoned social critique.

## The Takeaway

Italian Neorealism was born from catastrophe and lasted barely a decade, but it permanently widened what cinema could be *about* and *how* it could be made. Its deepest lesson is not a technique but an attitude: ordinary life, observed with patience and honesty, is dramatic enough. That attitude was never fully achievable in pure form, and the films themselves are full of artifice. But as an aspiration, it has proven one of the most durable and generative ideas in the history of the medium.
