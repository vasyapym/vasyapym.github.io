# Cannabis: A First-Principles Introduction

## Part One: The Plant

Cannabis is a flowering plant in the family *Cannabaceae*. Its closest well-known relative is *Humulus lupulus*, the hop plant used to bitter beer. The two diverged millions of years ago but still share a family resemblance in both leaf structure and aromatic chemistry. Most botanists now treat cannabis as a single, highly variable species, *Cannabis sativa* L. The familiar names "indica" and "ruderalis" are better understood as subspecies, varieties, or simply marketing labels. We will return to that controversy, because it illustrates how folk taxonomy and scientific taxonomy can drift apart.

Cannabis is typically **dioecious**: individual plants are either male or female. This matters because the compounds people care about are concentrated almost entirely in the female flowers, specifically in microscopic structures called **glandular trichomes**. Under magnification, these look like tiny glass mushrooms, a stalk topped with a bulbous head. That head is a biochemical factory. It secretes a sticky resin dense with cannabinoids and aromatic terpenes, probably evolved as a defense against herbivores, insects, and ultraviolet radiation.

If a female plant is pollinated, it diverts energy into producing seeds. If it remains unpollinated, it keeps producing resinous flowers in an attempt to catch pollen that never arrives. Growers exploit this by removing male plants, yielding **sinsemilla** (Spanish for "without seed"). In a sense, much of modern cannabis cultivation is a deliberate act of botanical frustration.

The legal world draws a line through this one species that biology does not recognize. **Hemp** and **marijuana** are the same plant. The distinction is regulatory: in the United States and many other jurisdictions, cannabis containing no more than 0.3% Δ9-THC by dry weight counts as hemp. That threshold traces back to a 1976 taxonomic paper by Ernest Small and Arthur Cronquist, who proposed it as a convenient dividing line, not a natural boundary. A somewhat arbitrary academic cutoff has become the foundation of billion-dollar industries and criminal statutes.

## Part Two: The Chemistry

### Cannabinoids

The defining chemicals of cannabis are the **phytocannabinoids**, a class of terpenophenolic compounds. More than a hundred have been identified, but two dominate the conversation:

- **Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)**, the principal intoxicating compound.
- **Cannabidiol (CBD)**, which is non-intoxicating in the conventional sense but pharmacologically active.

The plant does not actually make THC or CBD directly. It makes their **acidic precursors**. The biosynthetic pathway is elegant. The plant combines olivetolic acid with geranyl pyrophosphate to form **cannabigerolic acid (CBGA)**, sometimes called the "mother cannabinoid." Specialized enzymes then convert CBGA into one of several products:

- *THCA synthase* produces THCA.
- *CBDA synthase* produces CBDA.
- *CBCA synthase* produces CBCA.

Which enzymes a plant expresses, and how actively, is largely genetic. This is why some plants are THC-dominant and others CBD-dominant.

THCA is not meaningfully intoxicating. It must lose a carboxyl group, released as CO₂, to become THC. This reaction is called **decarboxylation**, and heat drives it. This is why smoking or vaporizing works immediately, and why eating raw cannabis flower produces little intoxication. It is also why cannabis intended for edibles must be heated first. If you have ever wondered why people bake cannabis in an oven before infusing butter, this is the reason: they are running a decarboxylation reaction in the kitchen.

THC also degrades over time, particularly with exposure to heat, light, and oxygen, oxidizing into **cannabinol (CBN)**. Old, poorly stored cannabis is therefore chemically different from fresh cannabis, which is part of why CBN has a folk reputation as "sleepy." The evidence for that reputation is thin.

A brief historical note: THC's structure was elucidated in 1964 by Raphael Mechoulam and Yechiel Gaoni in Israel. Mechoulam is often called the father of cannabinoid science, and his name will appear again.

### Terpenes

The smell of cannabis comes mostly from **terpenes**, volatile aromatic hydrocarbons found throughout the plant kingdom. Common ones include:

- **Myrcene**: earthy, musky; also found in mango and hops.
- **Limonene**: citrus.
- **α- and β-pinene**: pine, as the name suggests.
- **Linalool**: floral; prominent in lavender.
- **β-caryophyllene**: peppery, spicy.

β-Caryophyllene deserves special mention for geeks. It binds directly to the CB2 cannabinoid receptor, which arguably makes it a dietary cannabinoid. It is abundant in black pepper. So in a narrow technical sense, you have been consuming cannabinoid-receptor ligands at dinner your whole life.

## Part Three: The Endocannabinoid System

Here is where the story becomes biologically profound. Cannabis works because it mimics something your body already does.

### Discovery

Scientists long assumed THC acted nonspecifically, perhaps by dissolving into cell membranes like an anesthetic. That assumption collapsed when the research arrived in sequence:

- **1988**: Allyn Howlett and William Devane identified a specific binding site in the brain.
- **1990**: The **CB1 receptor** was cloned.
- **1993**: A second receptor, **CB2**, was identified, mainly in immune tissue.

The existence of receptors raised an obvious question. The human brain did not evolve receptors to wait for a plant. What is the body's own key for this lock?

The answer came in 1992, when Mechoulam's group, with Devane, isolated **anandamide**, from the Sanskrit *ananda*, meaning "bliss." A second major endocannabinoid, **2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG)**, followed in 1995. Together, these molecules, their receptors, and the enzymes that make and break them form the **endocannabinoid system (ECS)**.

### How it works

Most neurotransmitters travel *forward* across a synapse, from the sending neuron to the receiving one. Endocannabinoids are unusual: they are **retrograde messengers**. When a receiving (postsynaptic) neuron is strongly activated, it synthesizes endocannabinoids on demand from membrane lipids. These molecules diffuse *backward* to the sending (presynaptic) neuron, where they activate CB1 receptors and **reduce further neurotransmitter release**.

An analogy helps. Imagine a speaker (the presynaptic neuron) talking to a listener (the postsynaptic neuron). Normally information flows one way. The ECS gives the listener a way to say "okay, I hear you, you can lower your voice." It is a feedback dimmer switch, a system for fine-tuning signaling intensity throughout the brain.

Because endocannabinoids are made on demand and quickly degraded, their action is local and brief. Anandamide is broken down mainly by the enzyme **FAAH**, and 2-AG mainly by **MAGL**. The system works with spatial and temporal precision.

### Why THC is different

THC hijacks this machinery, and it does so clumsily. Rather than being produced at specific synapses for milliseconds, it floods the whole brain for hours and activates CB1 receptors indiscriminately. It is like replacing a skilled sound engineer, who adjusts individual microphones, with someone who turns every dimmer in the building down at once.

CB1 is one of the most abundant G-protein-coupled receptors in the brain. Its distribution maps neatly onto THC's effects:

- **Hippocampus** → impaired short-term memory.
- **Basal ganglia and cerebellum** → altered motor coordination and timing.
- **Prefrontal cortex** → changes in executive function and perception of time.
- **Hypothalamus** → appetite stimulation (the "munchies").
- **Amygdala** → modulation of anxiety and fear, in either direction.

Equally important is where CB1 is *sparse*: the **brainstem regions controlling breathing**. Opioid overdose kills largely by suppressing respiration, because opioid receptors are dense there. THC lacks this mechanism, which is the main reason there is no well-established case of death from THC overdose alone. That does not make cannabis harmless. People can still be severely harmed by accidents, panic, cardiovascular events in vulnerable individuals, or contaminated products. But the pharmacology of lethality is fundamentally different from opioids or alcohol.

THC is a **partial agonist** at CB1: it activates the receptor, but not maximally. This becomes crucial when comparing it to synthetic cannabinoids such as JWH-018, sold as "Spice" or "K2." Many of these are **full agonists** with much higher potency. That difference helps explain why they are associated with seizures, kidney injury, severe psychosis, and deaths that natural cannabis rarely or never causes. "Synthetic marijuana" is a dangerously misleading name.

### CBD's complicated pharmacology

CBD binds CB1 and CB2 only weakly. It is pharmacologically promiscuous, interacting with serotonin 5-HT1A receptors, TRPV1 channels, and several other targets. In laboratory studies it acts as a negative allosteric modulator of CB1, meaning it may alter how the receptor responds to THC, which is one hypothesis for why CBD might temper some of THC's effects. The clinical evidence for this tempering is mixed.

CBD has one firmly established medical use: the prescription drug **Epidiolex**, approved in 2018 for seizures associated with Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis complex. The effective doses in those trials were high, often hundreds to over a thousand milligrams daily. That is far beyond the few milligrams found in many retail CBD products. Most consumer CBD claims run well ahead of the evidence.

## Part Four: Pharmacokinetics, or How Route Changes Everything

The same molecule behaves very differently depending on how it enters the body. This is one of the most practically important concepts in the field.

**Inhalation** (smoking or vaporizing) delivers THC through the lungs directly into arterial blood, reaching the brain within seconds to minutes. Effects peak quickly and generally fade over two to four hours. Bioavailability is variable, roughly 10–35%, depending on technique.

**Oral ingestion** (edibles) is slower and stranger. Bioavailability is low, around 4–12%, and onset typically takes 30 minutes to 2 hours, sometimes longer. The THC passes through the liver first (**first-pass metabolism**), where the enzyme CYP2C9 converts much of it into **11-hydroxy-THC**. This metabolite is at least as potent as THC and appears to cross into the brain efficiently. As a result, edibles often feel qualitatively different: more body-heavy, longer-lasting (six to eight hours or more), and harder to predict.

This creates a classic trap. A person eats an edible, feels nothing after 45 minutes, takes more, and is then hit by both doses at once. A large share of cannabis-related emergency visits follow this pattern. The standard harm-reduction guidance, "start low, go slow," is grounded directly in pharmacokinetics.

THC is also highly **lipophilic** (fat-soluble). It accumulates in fatty tissue and leaches back out slowly. This is why its inactive metabolite, THC-COOH, can show up in urine for days in occasional users and for weeks in heavy daily users, long after any impairment has ended. It also explains why blood THC levels correlate poorly with actual impairment, a serious problem for designing roadside drug tests.

## Part Five: The Indica/Sativa Problem

Walk into a dispensary and you will likely see products labeled *indica* ("relaxing, body high"), *sativa* ("energizing, cerebral"), or *hybrid*. This framework is intuitive, popular, and largely unsupported as a predictor of effects.

The terms originally described plant **morphology**. Indica-type plants were shorter and bushier with broad leaves; sativa-type plants were taller with narrow leaves. After decades of crossbreeding, these lineages are thoroughly mixed. Genetic studies, including a notable 2015 analysis by Sawler and colleagues, found that a strain's commercial label often fails to match its actual genetic ancestry. Strain names themselves are also inconsistent: two products sold as the same name can differ chemically.

A more scientific approach classifies cannabis by **chemotype** (or "chemovar"), meaning its actual chemical profile:

- **Type I**: THC-dominant.
- **Type II**: mixed THC and CBD.
- **Type III**: CBD-dominant.

Additional types exist for plants dominated by rarer cannabinoids such as CBG. Terpene profiles may add further nuance, though how much they shape subjective effects is still uncertain.

This connects to the **entourage effect**, a hypothesis proposed by Mechoulam and colleagues in 1998 and later popularized by Ethan Russo. It suggests that the plant's many compounds act synergistically, so that whole-plant cannabis differs meaningfully from isolated THC. The idea is plausible and widely cited, but rigorous human evidence remains limited and mixed. Treat it as an open research question, not established fact.

## Part Six: Effects, Risks, and Medicine

### Acute effects

Typical effects of THC include euphoria, relaxation, altered perception of time, heightened sensory appreciation, increased appetite, impaired short-term memory and coordination, elevated heart rate, dry mouth, and reddened eyes (from blood vessel dilation).

Many of these effects are **biphasic**: low and high doses can produce opposite results. Anxiety is the best example. Modest doses often reduce it, while high doses can provoke anxiety, panic, or paranoia. The amygdala, rich in CB1 receptors, seems to sit at the center of this two-sided response.

### Potency has changed

A key modern fact is that cannabis is much stronger than it used to be. Average THC content in U.S. seized samples rose from roughly 4% in the mid-1990s to well above 15% by the late 2010s. Concentrates such as wax, shatter, and distillate commonly exceed 60–90%. Much older research on cannabis risk was conducted with weaker products, so its conclusions may not transfer cleanly to today's market.

### Risks

A balanced picture acknowledges real harms alongside the absence of lethal overdose:

- **Cannabis use disorder.** Estimates vary by definition and population, but a meaningful fraction of users, often cited as around one in ten, and substantially more among daily users or those who start in adolescence, develop problematic, compulsive use. Withdrawal is real but usually mild: irritability, insomnia, vivid dreams, reduced appetite.
- **Tolerance.** Repeated use causes CB1 receptor downregulation. Encouragingly, receptor availability largely recovers within about four weeks of abstinence.
- **Psychosis.** There is a consistent, dose-dependent association between heavy cannabis use, especially daily use of high-potency products, and psychotic disorders, with a major 2019 study in *The Lancet Psychiatry* among the key evidence. Individuals with a personal or family history of psychosis are at particular risk.
- **The adolescent brain.** The ECS plays a role in brain development, which continues into the mid-twenties. Early, heavy use is associated with poorer outcomes, though separating cause from correlation remains difficult.
- **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS).** This is a paradoxical condition in some long-term heavy users, marked by cyclical severe vomiting, often temporarily relieved by hot showers. The only reliable cure is stopping cannabis use.
- **Impaired driving.** THC measurably impairs reaction time and attention, and the risk compounds when combined with alcohol.
- **Accidental ingestion.** Pediatric poisonings from edibles that look like candy have risen sharply where cannabis is legal.
- **Respiratory effects.** Smoking is associated with chronic bronchitis symptoms. The link to lung cancer is less clear than with tobacco.

### Medicine

The 2017 report from the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine remains a useful benchmark. It found conclusive or substantial evidence for cannabis or cannabinoids in three areas:

1. Treating chronic pain in adults.
2. Treating chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.
3. Improving patient-reported spasticity in multiple sclerosis.

Approved cannabinoid pharmaceuticals include **dronabinol** and **nabilone** (synthetic THC and a THC analog), **nabiximols** (Sativex; approved in many countries but not the U.S.), and **Epidiolex** (CBD). Many other claimed benefits remain under investigation or are poorly supported.

## Summary: Key Concepts to Retain

1. **One species, many uses.** Hemp and marijuana are the same plant, divided by a legal THC threshold rather than biology.
2. **Acids become actives.** The plant makes THCA and CBDA. Heat converts them to THC and CBD through decarboxylation.
3. **Cannabis mimics an internal system.** The endocannabinoid system is a retrograde, on-demand feedback network that fine-tunes neural signaling. THC activates it broadly and for hours.
4. **Receptor maps explain effects.** CB1 density explains the memory, timing, and appetite effects, and its scarcity in brainstem breathing centers explains the lack of fatal overdose.
5. **Route matters enormously.** Inhaled effects are fast and short. Edible effects are slow, long, and potent because of 11-hydroxy-THC.
6. **Labels mislead; chemistry informs.** Indica and sativa poorly predict effects. Chemotype, meaning actual cannabinoid and terpene content, is more meaningful.
7. **Low lethality does not mean no harm.** Dependence, psychosis risk, CHS, impaired driving, and developmental concerns are real, especially with today's high-potency products.
