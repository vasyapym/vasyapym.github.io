# Akira Kurosawa: a working vocabulary, from beginner to specialist

## The basic map

Akira Kurosawa (1910–1998) directed films from Sanshiro Sugata in 1943 to Madadayo in 1993. He is often introduced as the director of Seven Samurai and Rashomon, or simply as a maker of samurai films. Both descriptions are too narrow. His work also includes crime stories, medical dramas, literary adaptations, studies of bureaucracy, and films about nuclear fear. A useful question to carry through all of them is: How can people see clearly enough to act responsibly when fear, self-interest, social rank, and violence distort what they know? That question is not a formula for every film, but it opens many of them.

Two Japanese genre terms establish the terrain. Jidaigeki means period drama; gendaigeki means drama set in contemporary life. Chanbara is the sword-fighting branch of period cinema, not a synonym for all jidaigeki. A rōnin is a samurai without a master, a precarious figure central to Seven Samurai and Yojimbo. Sengoku denotes Japan’s age of civil warfare, the setting or historical model for several Kurosawa epics. These films are interpretations of history, not transparent records of it. The word bushidō, often rendered “the way of the warrior,” also deserves caution: it does not name one timeless code that every historical samurai followed. Kurosawa is interested precisely in the distance between ideals of martial honor and the violence of class power.

His career crossed wartime censorship, the US-led Occupation, postwar reconstruction, economic expansion, and nuclear anxiety. Those conditions matter to the films’ meanings. Drunken Angel is inseparable from the wreckage of postwar urban life; I Live in Fear gives nuclear dread a domestic and institutional form; High and Low turns economic inequality into visible geography. To call Kurosawa a “humanist” can be useful if it means an attention to suffering and to the possibility of responsible action. It becomes misleading if it implies easy optimism.

Kurosawa is also a productive test of auteur theory, the practice of studying a director’s recurring concerns and formal decisions. His style is recognizable, but it was made collaboratively. Screenwriters including Shinobu Hashimoto, Hideo Oguni, and Ryūzō Kikushima; actors such as Toshirō Mifune and Takashi Shimura; cinematographers including Kazuo Miyagawa and Asakazu Nakai; and composers such as Fumio Hayasaka, Masaru Satō, and later Tōru Takemitsu were crucial collaborators. Kurosawa was a former aspiring painter and prepared elaborate visual plans, particularly for his later color films. Neither “solitary genius” nor “mere technician” adequately describes his work.

## The visual and sonic vocabulary

Mise-en-scène means the arrangement of people, objects, light, and space within a shot. Blocking is the movement and positioning of actors within that arrangement. These terms are especially valuable for Kurosawa because a scene’s argument may happen before anyone speaks. In the first portion of High and Low, several people occupy a wealthy executive’s living room as a kidnapping crisis develops. Watching who stands apart, who approaches the telephone, and who must remain waiting reveals the distribution of authority and vulnerability. A widescreen composition can let those relationships develop within one image rather than assigning every character a separate close-up.

Staging in depth places consequential action at different distances from the camera. Deep focus, when used, keeps those distances relatively clear to the eye. Kurosawa also employed long, or telephoto, lenses, often from farther camera positions. That combination can make people at different distances appear pressed together in the image. “Kurosawa uses deep space” and “Kurosawa compresses space” are not competing universal descriptions: they name different choices for different scenes. The specialist’s question is what a particular choice permits us to notice—or prevents us from separating.

A cut changes instantly from one shot to another. Montage is the construction of meaning and rhythm through the relationship between shots; it need not mean merely “fast editing.” An axial cut moves closer to or farther from a subject along approximately the same line of sight, producing an abrupt change in scale without a conventional change of viewing angle. A wipe replaces one image with another across a moving boundary. Kurosawa’s wipes are conspicuous and often energetic, but they are one device within a larger rhythmic practice: he can hold a composition, cut with an actor’s movement, accelerate into action, and then make us sit with its aftermath.

Beginning with Seven Samurai, he used multiple cameras extensively for complex action. Simultaneous views of riders, crowds, mud, and rain could be assembled without requiring every unpredictable movement to be repeated identically. The achievement is not simply that action looks turbulent. Much of the time, we can grasp the village’s layout, the direction of an attack, and what is at stake at a particular opening in the defenses. Tactical clarity makes danger intelligible. When the action becomes difficult to see, that difficulty has dramatic force because we have some prior sense of the space being lost.

Movement in a Kurosawa shot occurs on several levels: actors cross the frame, cameras pan or travel, groups surge, and wind, rain, fog, dust, or snow alter the visible world. Weather is therefore not just atmosphere or a fixed symbolic code. Rain at the gate in Rashomon obscures and isolates; the rain in Seven Samurai’s last battle makes bodies and decisions harder to control; fog in Throne of Blood turns familiar ground into uncertain territory. Each has a practical effect on what people can do and what viewers can discern.

Sound matters just as much. Diegetic sound belongs to the story world, such as a character’s voice or a song the characters can hear; nondiegetic music is supplied to the audience without an apparent source in that world. Kurosawa can place image and sound in counterpoint rather than have music merely repeat the image’s emotion. In Ikiru, a familiar song becomes devastating through the changed circumstances in which its protagonist sings it. In Ran, Takemitsu’s score and the suppression of much expected battle noise can make slaughter feel horrifyingly remote instead of exciting. Silence, song, noise, and orchestral music are decisions about our relation to an event.

## The films as problems to think with

Drunken Angel (1948) and Stray Dog (1949) make a good entrance to Kurosawa outside the samurai genre. The former places a physician and a gangster amid the physical and moral contamination of a postwar neighborhood. The latter follows a young detective whose stolen pistol draws him through a sweltering city. They borrow some of the darkness and pursuit associated with film noir, but the point is not merely stylish pessimism. Stray Dog asks how people exposed to comparable social damage take divergent paths, without pretending that hardship excuses harm. I Live in Fear (1955), about a man desperate to protect his family from nuclear catastrophe, likewise resists the easy division between rational society and irrational individual: the fear is understandable, while its consequences are destructive.

Rashomon (1950) is the essential vocabulary lesson in narrative perspective. A bandit, a woman, a dead samurai speaking through a medium, and a woodcutter provide incompatible accounts of an assault and death. The film draws on Ryūnosuke Akutagawa’s “In a Grove” for its testimony and “Rashōmon” for its ruined-gate setting. What is now called the “Rashomon effect” is the problem of conflicting accounts shaped by different interests and viewpoints. It does not mean that nothing happened, that all accounts are equally trustworthy, or that facts cannot exist. The forest scenes look vivid and immediate, yet their apparent photographic reality cannot certify the testimony they visualize. Even the woodcutter’s seemingly corrective account is compromised by the matter of a missing dagger. At the end, an act of care for an abandoned baby offers a limited reason to trust human action; it does not solve the crime retroactively. Nor should the sexual assault at the story’s center be reduced to an interchangeable philosophical puzzle: the witnesses do not occupy equal positions of power.

Seven Samurai (1954) is at once a gripping defense story and an inquiry into whether effective heroism changes an unjust society. Farmers recruit seven rōnin to protect their village from bandits. Kurosawa devotes time to recruitment, training, terrain, barriers, and the rhythms of village work; the climactic fighting matters because the preparations have made its costs legible. Mifune’s Kikuchiyo, a farmer-born man passing as a samurai, exposes the hostility beneath the alliance. The villagers have good reason to fear warriors, and the warriors’ self-image does not erase what their class has done to peasants. The closing contrast between the surviving samurai and the farmers returning to their fields is fundamental. A battle can be won without abolishing the hierarchy that made it necessary.

Ikiru (1952), meaning “To Live,” exchanges military tactics for municipal paperwork. A civil servant, Watanabe, learns that he is dying and struggles to bring about a children’s playground. The film does not stop with his private awakening. After his death, colleagues at his wake reconstruct his final efforts, distribute and withdraw credit, promise to change, and confront the pull of institutional habit. Its structure asks whether a good act can endure inside a system skilled at absorbing it. Watanabe’s song on a swing in the snow is moving not because it proves he conquered death or bureaucracy, but because a modest, material improvement has been wrested from both.

Throne of Blood (1957) transposes Shakespeare’s Macbeth into a Japanese military world. Its original title, Kumonosu-jō, means “Spider’s Web Castle.” Noh-influenced stillness, masklike expression, measured movement, forest fog, and sudden arrow violence make ambition feel less like an isolated personality defect than entrapment in a system of prophecy and force. Lady Asaji’s controlled bearing is particularly unsettling beside Washizu’s agitation. “Kurosawa adapts Shakespeare” is thus only the start of the analysis: the interesting question is what changes when a familiar dramatic problem is rebuilt through another performance tradition and political landscape.

Yojimbo (1961) and Sanjuro (1962) display the pleasure and danger of charismatic swordsmanship. Their wandering protagonist uses shrewdness as well as skill, but the films do not make violence innocent simply because he is good at it. Sanjuro’s spectacular final burst of blood can be read as an excess that jars against the apparent neatness of a duel. The Hidden Fortress (1958), meanwhile, often filters an epic adventure through two quarrelsome, low-status travelers. Point of view matters: the story of powerful figures changes when people who barely understand their plans guide our entrance into it.

High and Low (1963), adapted from Ed McBain’s King’s Ransom, begins when a kidnapper mistakes a chauffeur’s child for the son of a shoe-company executive, Gondo. Paying the ransom may wreck Gondo’s financial plans. The film moves from an intensely staged decision in his hillside home into a painstaking police investigation through the city below. Its Japanese title, Tengoku to jigoku, means “Heaven and Hell”: social height and depth are literal features of the setting as well as moral provocations. The isolated pink smoke in an otherwise black-and-white film is a striking clue, but the investigation’s accumulation of evidence cannot by itself resolve the inequality the camera has shown. Gondo is neither simply “a bad rich man” nor someone whose costly choice makes his economic world just.

Kagemusha (1980), meaning “Shadow Warrior,” follows a double whose resemblance sustains the authority of a dead warlord. Costume, ceremony, and collective belief become instruments of rule: political identity appears both materially consequential and precariously theatrical. Ran (1985), meaning “Chaos,” reworks King Lear through a warlord, Hidetora, and the sons among whom he divides his power. Its vast color-coded armies can look magnificent, but that beauty is unstable; the spectacle is a picture of ruin. Hidetora’s suffering cannot be understood apart from the suffering he earlier inflicted. These films ask us to examine our pleasure in images of martial grandeur even as the narratives expose its human cost.

Red Beard (1965), Kurosawa’s last film with Mifune and his last in black and white, follows a young physician learning to care for impoverished patients. It makes compassion look like sustained attention and work, though its powerful mentor figure also invites questions about benevolent authority. Dodes’ka-den (1970), his first color film, turns to an ensemble of people living in poverty. Dersu Uzala (1975), made with Soviet collaborators, addresses friendship, environmental knowledge, and loss through a Russian explorer’s relationship with an Indigenous hunter. The later films, including Dreams (1990) and Madadayo (1993), return to memory, mortality, war, and the nonhuman world. It would be too simple to describe this career as a steady slide from early hope to late despair: warmth and catastrophe coexist throughout it.

## The larger questions

The most useful advanced distinction is between seeing, knowing, and doing. Rashomon makes testimony unstable; Seven Samurai grants tactical knowledge without delivering class justice; Ikiru gives a man certainty of death without automatically giving him purpose; High and Low builds an impressive body of evidence without curing social division; Ran offers panoramic views of consequences no ruler can master. Kurosawa is neither saying “truth is impossible” nor assuring us that competent, decent individuals can fix a society. He repeatedly tests the gap between insight and effective responsibility.

That gap also helps explain the tension in his humanism. His films value courage, care, and collective labor, yet are drawn to exceptional men whose skill commands the screen. The samurai who saves a village may still belong to an oppressive class. A compassionate doctor may teach through paternal authority. Careful viewing keeps both sides present rather than awarding a film one uncomplicated political label. It should likewise ask who receives a voice: women’s perspectives in films such as Rashomon and Ran cannot be treated merely as functions of male honor or succession. Questions about Japanese wartime aggression and Japanese suffering also matter to readings of his later work on war and nuclear memory.

Finally, Kurosawa’s internationalism is more interesting than the claim that he was “the Western Japanese director.” The Idiot adapts Dostoevsky; The Lower Depths adapts Gorky; The Bad Sleep Well has Hamlet-like elements; Throne of Blood and Ran transform Shakespeare; High and Low reworks an American crime novel. In the other direction, Seven Samurai became a model for The Magnificent Seven, Yojimbo for A Fistful of Dollars, and George Lucas has acknowledged The Hidden Fortress as an influence on Star Wars. Influence travels in several directions. These works are neither less Japanese for adapting foreign material nor reducible to examples of a supposedly timeless “Japanese spirit.”

For a first viewing route, try Rashomon for testimony, Ikiru for time and bureaucracy, Seven Samurai for space and collective action, High and Low for widescreen staging and class, Throne of Blood for adaptation, and Ran for the later color epics. On a second viewing, ask a concrete question at each scene: Who controls the account? Where are bodies placed in relation to one another? What does a cut let me know? What can a character know but not change? For deeper study, Donald Richie’s The Films of Akira Kurosawa, Stephen Prince’s The Warrior’s Camera, and Mitsuhiro Yoshimoto’s Kurosawa: Film Studies and Japanese Cinema offer differing ways into the films and their critical history.
