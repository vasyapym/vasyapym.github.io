# Roman Polanski: The Concepts and Vocabulary

Orientation

Roman Polanski was born in Paris in August 1933 to Polish parents of Jewish heritage. In 1937 the family returned to Kraków, and after the German invasion of 1939 it was forced into the Kraków ghetto. His mother was murdered at Auschwitz. His father was deported to Mauthausen and survived. The boy slipped out of the ghetto around the time of its liquidation and lived out the war hidden among Polish families, often in the countryside, passing as a Catholic. After 1945 he became a child actor in radio, theatre and film, appearing in Andrzej Wajda's A Generation (1955). He then entered the state film school at Łódź, an institution that under communism was both cage and sanctuary: closely supervised, but rich in equipment, foreign films and time.

His student shorts already carry the signature. In Two Men and a Wardrobe (1958), two men walk out of the sea carrying a large wardrobe. The town they enter rebuffs and beats them, and they walk back into the sea. In Mammals (1962), two men share one sledge across a snowfield and spend the film cheating each other over who gets to ride.

His first feature, Knife in the Water (1962), was made in Poland and nominated for the Academy Award for best foreign-language film; it put three people on a small yacht. A British period followed in the mid-1960s, then a Hollywood period culminating in Chinatown (1974). After 1978 came a long European, mostly French, career that continued to The Palace (2023), released when he was ninety.

The films span psychological horror, Shakespeare, film noir, Thomas Hardy, a Holocaust memoir, drawing-room comedy and political thriller. Yet critics routinely describe the oeuvre (a creator's body of work considered as a whole) as one of the most internally consistent in postwar cinema. The reason is structural rather than generic. Again and again Polanski assembles the same machine: a handful of people, a bounded space, a pressure that rises, a shift in who holds power, and an ending that refuses release. The vocabulary below is the language critics have built to describe that machine.

Enclosure: The Apartment and the Mind

The most basic idea in Polanski criticism is claustrophobic space. The term means a setting staged so that walls, ceilings and doorways seem to press in on a character, and through the character on the viewer. Claustrophobia here is not merely a small set. It is a relation between space and mind in which the room becomes a picture of the psyche, the inner life of the person inside it. When the space deforms, we understand that the mind is deforming. When the space seals, we understand that the mind has nowhere left to go.

The canonical demonstration is Repulsion (1965). It was shot in London with Catherine Deneuve as Carol, a young Belgian manicurist living with her sister in a South Kensington flat. When the sister leaves for a holiday with her married lover, Carol stays behind and the flat begins to change. A skinned rabbit left out for dinner rots on a plate. Potatoes sprout. Cracks split the plaster with a report like a gunshot. In the film's most famous image, hands burst from the corridor walls and grope at her as she passes.

Polanski never cuts away to a doctor or a sympathetic outsider who could tell us what is real. We are locked inside with her. By the time she kills a suitor and then her landlord, the apartment has become indistinguishable from her breakdown. The final shot tracks slowly into a family photograph and finds the child Carol staring away from the group. Her expression may hint at an origin for her terror, or may only tempt us to invent one.

From this comes the notion of entrapment as worldview. In most genres a trap is a plot device, escaped in the third act. In Polanski the trap is closer to a statement about the human condition. Consider the tidal island of Cul-de-sac (1966), the yacht of Knife in the Water, the single living room of Carnage (2011), the bleak island house of The Ghost Writer (2010), and the Warsaw rooms where the hero hides in The Pianist (2002). Exits exist, but they lead to other enclosures. Critics use the term to separate suspense, which promises a resolution, from dread, which promises only continuation.

The Apartment Trilogy is the label critics attach to Repulsion, Rosemary's Baby (1968) and The Tenant (1976). In all three, a lone tenant in a city apartment comes to believe that neighbours, or the building itself, are conspiring against them. Rosemary's Baby was made in New York from Ira Levin's novel. Mia Farrow's Rosemary moves with her actor husband into the Bramford, a Gothic apartment house, and becomes pregnant amid the attentions of elderly neighbours whose solicitude grows suffocating. The Tenant was made in Paris from a novel by Roland Topor. Polanski himself plays Trelkovsky, a shy Polish-born clerk who rents a flat whose previous occupant threw herself from the window. He slowly comes to believe the other residents are remaking him into her.

The label is useful because it isolates a genuine pattern. The apartment works as a membrane between private self and hostile society. Paranoia, a fixed conviction of persecution, becomes both subject and viewing experience.

The label is misleading in three ways. First, Polanski did not conceive the films as a series; the grouping is retrospective. Second, they resolve their ambiguities differently. Repulsion leaves no doubt that Carol is ill, Rosemary's Baby ultimately confirms that Rosemary was right, and The Tenant refuses to decide. Third, the label quarantines a theme that saturates the whole career, implying the apartment is a phase rather than a permanent condition of the work. Used carefully, the trilogy is a teaching cluster, not a canon.

The Absurd

Absurdism names a philosophical position most associated with Albert Camus's The Myth of Sisyphus (1942). Human beings crave meaning in a universe that offers none, and the clash between the two is the basic fact of existence. In theatre, the critic Martin Esslin gathered playwrights such as Samuel Beckett and Eugène Ionesco under the phrase Theatre of the Absurd, in his 1961 book of that name. These are plays in which language breaks down, action circles without progress, and comedy and despair become the same gesture.

Polanski came of age when such drama was performed and argued over in Poland's comparatively open theatre culture after 1956, and his shorts are close cousins of that stage. The wardrobe carried out of and back into the sea is a pure absurdist image. It is a burden without explanation, carried with comic earnestness, rejected by society, and returned to nothing.

The feature-length statement is Cul-de-sac, shot on Holy Island off the Northumberland coast. Two wounded gangsters stumble into a castle occupied by a timid husband, played by Donald Pleasence, and his bored young French wife, played by Françoise Dorléac. The surviving gangster spends the film waiting for a boss named Katelbach to rescue him. Katelbach never comes; the debt to Beckett's Waiting for Godot, first staged in 1953, is barely concealed. Characters humiliate one another in long, uncomfortable routines. The tide cuts the island off and reconnects it. The film ends with the husband alone on a rock as the sea rises, calling the name of his first wife.

Critics use "absurd" of Polanski in two senses that should be kept apart. The tonal sense means deadpan, cruel, circular comedy. The metaphysical sense means a world without guarantees. Most of his films have the second even when they lack the first.

The Kafkaesque and Nightmare Logic

Kafkaesque, after Franz Kafka, describes a situation in which a helpless individual is caught in a system whose rules are never disclosed. Its officials are courteous and implacable, and its verdict seems to precede any crime.

One common confusion should be cleared at once: Polanski never filmed Kafka. The Trial (1962), sometimes listed alongside his early work, is Orson Welles's adaptation, released the same year as Knife in the Water. It is a useful comparison because the two directors take opposite routes. Welles renders Kafka as baroque architecture, with vast offices and cavernous halls. Polanski's Kafkaesque is domestic and small.

The exemplary case is The Tenant. Neighbours reproach Trelkovsky for noise he is not making. They press him to sign a petition against another resident, and the concierge scolds him. Gradually he comes to feel that the building has a will of its own, one he keeps failing to obey. No one ever states the rule. That is the method: the system is inscrutable not because it is complicated but because it is social, a web of disapproval impossible to argue with.

Closely related is nightmare logic, a term borrowed from the description of dreams. It means a narrative in which events follow emotional rather than causal connection, so that the impossible arrives with a dream's flat inevitability. In The Tenant, Trelkovsky finds a human tooth hidden in a hole in the wall. Across the courtyard he sees figures standing motionless in the communal toilet. Polanski frames these moments as calmly as a breakfast scene.

Nightmare logic matters because it produces the central problem of delusion versus reality: is what we see the world, or a mind's distortion of it? Rosemary's Baby is a masterclass in sustaining that question. Almost every sinister event has a plausible innocent explanation, from the odd taste of a neighbour's chocolate mousse to the doctor who dismisses Rosemary's pain. The film keeps open the possibility of ordinary prenatal anxiety until its last scene shuts that door.

The ambiguity is not decoration. It is how Polanski makes the viewer share the protagonist's isolation: no one, including us, can be sure enough to help.

Identity, Doubling and the Uncanny

Identity dissolution describes the gradual erosion of a character's sense of who they are, until the self merges with another person, a role, or nothing. Its most literal form is the doppelgänger (German for "double-goer"), the figure of the double that haunts Romantic literature. The psychoanalyst Otto Rank studied it in a 1914 essay as an image of the self split by fear of death. Sigmund Freud's essay "The Uncanny" (1919) supplied the adjacent term. The uncanny is the eerie feeling produced when something familiar returns as strange, such as a doll that seems alive or a self encountered outside oneself.

The Tenant is Polanski's fullest study of the theme. Trelkovsky starts smoking the cigarettes the previous tenant smoked and drinking what she drank. Eventually he puts on her dress and makeup and throws himself, twice, from her window. The film ends in a hospital room we have seen before, where he lies bandaged as she did, screaming at visitors who include himself. It is a closed loop in which the self is not lost so much as replaced.

Doubling recurs in subtler forms. In Chinatown, Evelyn Mulwray's secret turns on a girl who is both her daughter and her sister. In The Ghost Writer, the unnamed narrator literally takes a dead predecessor's place: he sleeps in his room, follows his notes, drives his car along its last programmed route, and finally shares his fate. In Venus in Fur (2013), Mathieu Amalric, whose resemblance to the young Polanski critics have noted, plays a writer-director whose auditioning actress progressively takes over his play and his authority. Critics use doubling to name Polanski's recurring suspicion that identity is a costume others assign.

Black Comedy Fused with Horror

Black comedy is humour drawn from death, cruelty or taboo. Polanski's distinctive move is to fuse it with horror rather than alternate the two, so that a scene is funny and frightening at the same instant.

The Fearless Vampire Killers (1967), released in some markets as Dance of the Vampires, is the explicit case. A bumbling professor and his timid assistant, played by Polanski, hunt vampires in a snowbound castle. Among the jokes is a Jewish vampire on whom a crucifix has no effect. The film ends with the heroes believing they have triumphed while unknowingly carrying vampirism out into the world.

Rosemary's Baby works the same seam less obviously. Its Satanists are fussy, garrulous, elderly New Yorkers. Ruth Gordon's Minnie Castevet, in a performance that won an Academy Award, is as comic as she is sinister.

Critics stress the fusion because it disables the usual safety valve. Comic relief normally lets tension out. In Polanski, laughter becomes another sign that the world is indifferent to suffering.

Genre Pastiche and the Polanski Genre Film

Pastiche, in film criticism, means imitating a genre's or style's conventions, usually without the mockery that parody implies. "The Polanski genre film" is critical shorthand for his habit of entering an established form, honouring its rules, and then turning it toward his own preoccupations. The forms include horror, noir, Shakespearean tragedy, occult thriller and political thriller.

Chinatown is the central example and a founding text of neo-noir. The term covers films made after the classical film noir cycle of the 1940s and 1950s that consciously revive its private eyes, femmes fatales, urban corruption and fatalism, usually in colour and with fewer censorship constraints. Robert Towne's screenplay sends the private investigator J.J. Gittes, played by Jack Nicholson, into a 1937 Los Angeles conspiracy over water rights that opens onto incest and the theft of a city's future. The genre markers are deliberate, down to casting John Huston, director of The Maltese Falcon (1941), as the patriarch Noah Cross.

The classical detective restores order through knowledge, and Polanski inverts that role. Gittes appears in almost every scene, so we know only what he knows, and each discovery makes things worse. His interventions lead directly to Evelyn Mulwray's death.

According to widely reported accounts from those involved, Towne wanted a less bleak ending and Polanski insisted on the one we have. Evelyn is shot dead, her daughter is delivered to Cross, and Gittes is led away with the line "Forget it, Jake. It's Chinatown." Polanski also appears as the small thug who slits Gittes's nostril with a knife. The cameo literalises the price of looking too closely.

Macbeth (1971), co-written with the theatre critic Kenneth Tynan, treats Shakespeare as medieval horror of mud, rain and graphic violence. It adds an ending in which Donalbain, the murdered king's younger son, seeks out the witches, implying the cycle will begin again. The Ninth Gate (1999), adapted from Arturo Pérez-Reverte's novel The Club Dumas, sends Johnny Depp's mercenary rare-book dealer after a seventeenth-century manual for summoning the devil, and plays the occult thriller with dry irony. The Ghost Writer, from Robert Harris's novel The Ghost, is a political thriller about a writer hired to finish a former British prime minister's memoirs.

Critics value the Polanski genre film as a test of how personal vision survives commercial form. The recurring answer is that he treats genre as a container whose walls he can press against.

The Unsympathetic Protagonist and Misanthropy

A protagonist is the central character. Sympathy is the viewer's emotional alignment with that character, and many Polanski films withhold it or let it curdle. The husband in Knife in the Water is a bully. The husband in Cul-de-sac is pathetic. Gittes is vain and smug. The four parents of Carnage, confined to one apartment for the length of a single conversation, strip off their civility until all of them are ugly.

Critics often call this misanthropy, a general distrust of or contempt for humankind. The word is defensible if understood precisely. Polanski rarely sides with a villain. He declines to flatter anyone, including victims, and treats decency as a thin coating that pressure will crack.

The result is a different contract with the audience. Instead of identifying with a hero and being purged, we watch, judge, and catch ourselves judging.

Voyeurism and the Controlling Gaze

Voyeurism, in everyday use the pleasure of watching unseen, became central to film theory after Laura Mulvey's essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema" (1975). Mulvey argued that classical cinema organised looking around a controlling, usually male, gaze, a term for the power the one who looks holds over the one looked at. Polanski suits this vocabulary because his films are full of watchers and the watched.

Knife in the Water is a contest between two men performed before a woman. The husband shows off his seamanship, the young hitchhiker his knife tricks. The camera often stacks all three in a single deep-focus frame, sharp from foreground to background, so that every look is visible to the others.

In The Tenant, Trelkovsky watches the courtyard and becomes convinced it watches back, and looking turns from privilege into persecution. Where Alfred Hitchcock's Rear Window (1954) makes the watcher a detective, Polanski makes him the victim.

Critics divide over whether his camera critiques the controlling gaze or indulges it, especially in its treatment of women in Repulsion and Bitter Moon (1992). That disagreement is itself one of the live debates around the work.

Sound, Subjective Camera and Craft

Subjective camera means shots composed from, or close to, a character's point of view, so the viewer sees as the character sees. Polanski seldom holds a literal point-of-view shot for long. He prefers what narrative theorists call restricted focalization: the story is filtered through one character's knowledge and perception even when the camera stands slightly apart.

Repulsion is built this way. Its sound design, the deliberate arrangement of everything audible, does as much work as its images. A ticking clock, a dripping tap, the bells of a nearby convent and a buzzing fly are amplified until silence itself becomes pressure. In Rosemary's Baby, Krzysztof Komeda's score opens with a wordless lullaby sung by Mia Farrow, making innocence sound like a threat. Critics call such sound psychological because it is diegetic, issuing from the story world, yet scaled to a mind.

This connects to a craft ethic critics treat as a signature. Its elements are chamber casts (very small ensembles, by analogy with chamber music), precise blocking (the planned movement of actors within the frame), and a preference for achieving effects in camera, on set and through the lens, rather than in post-production. In Repulsion the groping hands were real hands pushed through a pliable wall, and the sets were altered so that rooms seemed to change proportion as Carol deteriorates. Cul-de-sac contains a celebrated unbroken take of several minutes on the beach, two actors talking while light and weather shift around them.

In Rosemary's Baby, Minnie makes a phone call in a bedroom seen through a doorway that half hides her. Polanski has recounted that audiences leaned sideways in their seats to see past the frame.

Mise-en-scène, the French term for everything placed within the frame and how it is arranged, is for Polanski not decoration but argument. What the frame excludes is as charged as what it shows.

The Outsider and Historical Trauma

The outsider is a figure who does not belong to the surrounding community, whether by nationality, class, temperament or fate. Polanski's outsiders are numerous: a Belgian in London, a Pole in Paris, a Jewish pianist in a city emptying of Jews, a detective who cannot get inside the story he investigates.

Historical trauma names the lasting psychic and cultural injury left by events such as war and genocide, which may surface indirectly in art made long afterward. For most of his career Polanski kept his own war off the screen.

The Pianist finally addressed it directly. Ronald Harwood adapted it from the memoir of the Warsaw musician Władysław Szpilman. It won the Palme d'Or at Cannes and Academy Awards for direction, adapted screenplay, and Adrien Brody's lead performance.

What is striking is how closely the film obeys the old grammar. Szpilman spends long stretches hiding alone in apartments outside the ghetto walls. Through windows he watches the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943 and later the Warsaw Uprising of 1944: confined, passive, a witness rather than an actor. Polanski has said that he drew on his own memories of the ghetto for details of the film.

The Pianist therefore invites critics to read backward. The apartment terrors of the 1960s and 1970s begin to look like trauma displaced into genre, and The Pianist like the source finally named.

Adaptation and the Production Periods

Most of Polanski's films are adaptations of a novel, play or memoir. Critics increasingly read his career as literature-first: the source supplies a structure that he compresses toward enclosure.

Tess (1979), from Thomas Hardy's Tess of the d'Urbervilles, is the expansive exception, a film of fields and seasons. It is dedicated to Sharon Tate, the actress who was Polanski's wife and who was murdered in 1969 by followers of Charles Manson. It was shot in France and won Academy Awards for cinematography, art direction and costume design.

Later work leans toward the theatre. Death and the Maiden (1994), from Ariel Dorfman's play, confines a former political prisoner and the man she believes tortured her in one house. Carnage, from Yasmina Reza's play, unfolds in near real time, with the story's duration matching the screen's. Venus in Fur, from David Ives's play riffing on Leopold von Sacher-Masoch's novella, is a two-hander, a work for two performers, set in an empty theatre. An Officer and a Spy (2019), from Robert Harris's novel about the Dreyfus affair, follows Georges Picquart as he uncovers the framing of a Jewish captain and is punished by the army he serves.

Context shaped style across the production periods. The Polish period, comprising the shorts and Knife in the Water, worked within a state system of censors, scarce resources and ideological scrutiny. Knife in the Water drew official disapproval for its cool, apolitical cynicism, and its spare means reinforced the economy of three people on a boat.

The English period of Repulsion, Cul-de-sac and later Macbeth, made with modest independent backing, added cosmopolitan freedom and an outsider's eye on British reserve. The American period of Rosemary's Baby and Chinatown brought studio resources, stars and the discipline of Hollywood genre. The European, chiefly French, period after 1978 returned him to modest budgets, multinational casts and theatrical sources. Each shift altered surface and scale; the underlying machine persisted.

The Biographical Controversy

In 1977, in Los Angeles, Polanski was charged with multiple offences involving a thirteen-year-old girl, including rape by use of drugs. Under a plea agreement he pleaded guilty to one charge, unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor, and the remaining charges were dropped. He spent forty-two days in a state prison undergoing psychiatric evaluation. In February 1978, saying he believed the judge was about to set aside the agreement and impose further prison time, he left for France before sentencing. France does not generally extradite its own citizens, and he has not returned to the United States.

In 2009 he was arrested in Zurich on an American warrant and held under house arrest at his chalet in Gstaad, where he completed The Ghost Writer. Switzerland declined extradition in 2010, and Polish courts rejected a later American request in 2015 and 2016. The victim, who has spoken publicly under her own name, has said she wants the case ended. Other women have since made allegations, which he denies. In 2018 he was expelled from the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences.

These facts shadow the films' reception. Festival honours have prompted protests, and some viewers and institutions decline to engage with the work at all. An honest account states them and then returns to the films.

Competing Critical Framings

The advanced conversation around Polanski concerns less what the films contain than what explains them. Four framings compete, and they are readings, not facts.

Trauma theory, developed in literary studies in the 1990s by scholars including Cathy Caruth, holds that overwhelming experience is not fully registered when it happens. It returns belatedly, through repetition, fragmentation and displacement rather than direct memory. Applied to Polanski, it reads the recurring enclosures, sudden violence and unstable perception as the form a ghetto childhood takes when it cannot be narrated. Repulsion becomes terror displaced onto a young woman in London. The Tenant becomes a fantasy of being persecuted by neighbours for one's foreignness. The Pianist becomes the belated return to the source. The reading's strength is its reach. Its weakness is that it can explain anything, and it risks converting deliberate choices into symptoms.

A second framing treats the absurd as a post-Holocaust sensibility. The European absurd has often been read as a response to a modernity whose rational, bureaucratic machinery produced mass death. In that world, as Theodor Adorno's well-known remark about poetry after Auschwitz is usually paraphrased, easy affirmation became suspect. On this view Polanski's black comedy is not cynicism but a historically earned refusal of consolation. Examples include the vampire a crucifix cannot repel, the detective whose knowledge kills, and the institution in An Officer and a Spy that persecutes a Jew to protect itself. The framing places Polanski within a generation of Central European artists shaped by the same history, rather than isolating him as a case.

A third debate concerns authorship. Auteur theory grew from the French critics around the journal Cahiers du cinéma in the 1950s and was popularised in America by Andrew Sarris. It treats the director as a film's true author, whose personal vision can be traced across works regardless of genre or studio. Polanski is a near-ideal auteurist subject, and this essay's organising claim of internal consistency is itself an auteurist claim.

Anti-auteurist readings push back by pointing to collaborators. Gérard Brach co-wrote many films from Repulsion onward. Robert Towne wrote Chinatown, and Komeda composed the early scores. Cinematographers including Gilbert Taylor, William Fraker, Sven Nykvist and Paweł Edelman shaped the images, and source authors from Levin to Harris supplied the stories. Roland Barthes's essay "The Death of the Author" (1967) provides the theoretical lever, shifting meaning from creator to text and reader. The anti-auteurist does not deny the style. The question is how much of the consistency belongs to Polanski and how much is produced by critics looking for it.

The fourth and most contested question is whether the style itself, its control, enclosure and precision, should be read biographically. One version links it to the ghetto: a child who survived by hiding learns to experience rooms as threat and refuge at once. Another version, sharpened by the 1977 case and later allegations, reads the recurring power games, sexual humiliations and controlling gaze as disclosures of their maker. Some critics now revisit Bitter Moon or Venus in Fur in that light.

Against both stands formalism, which prioritises form, structure and technique over biography. It insists that a precisely made film is evidence of craft, not confession, and that biographical decoding flattens works into alibis or indictments. Each position catches something. The trauma reading illuminates The Pianist. The formalist reading explains why Chinatown grips viewers who know nothing of its director. The critical-biographical reading names a discomfort many viewers feel. The mature stance is to hold these as competing lenses and to say which one is in use.

A Note on Method and Uncertainties

Three assumptions behind the usual account deserve challenge, and this essay has handled them openly. First, the Apartment Trilogy is a critics' grouping, not the director's design, and is treated here as a convenience. Second, the prompt-level habit of filing The Trial (1962) among Polanski's films is an error: it is Welles's film, and the Kafkaesque in Polanski is a method, not an adaptation. Third, internal consistency is an auteurist premise; the credible alternative, that collaborators, sources and critical habit generate much of the coherence, is presented above rather than dismissed.

Production anecdotes, such as the Chinatown ending dispute and audience reactions to the doorway shot, rest on participants' recollections and are attributed as such. Links between the war years and specific scenes depend on the director's own statements and remain interpretive. No critic is credited with a reading of Polanski unless the attribution is secure; broader positions are paraphrased.

Glossary

Absurd — The condition, described by Camus, of beings who seek meaning in a universe that supplies none. On stage and screen, circular, deadpan action in which comedy and despair coincide.

Apartment Trilogy — Critics' retrospective label for Repulsion, Rosemary's Baby and The Tenant. Useful for the apartment-as-psyche motif; misleading if taken as a planned series.

Auteur theory — The view that the director is a film's true author, with a vision traceable across works. Born among Cahiers du cinéma critics; contested by anti-auteurist and text-centred approaches.

Black comedy — Humour drawn from death, cruelty or taboo. In Polanski it is fused with horror, so that laughter offers no relief.

Chamber piece — A drama for a very small cast in a confined setting, by analogy with chamber music. Knife in the Water, Death and the Maiden and Carnage are examples.

Doppelgänger — The double: a second self who mirrors or replaces the first. Central to The Tenant and echoed in The Ghost Writer and Venus in Fur.

Entrapment — Structuring a story around a space or situation that cannot truly be escaped. In Polanski it functions as a worldview rather than a mere plot device.

Focalization — The filtering of a narrative through one character's perception and knowledge. Restricted focalization lets Repulsion and Chinatown know only what their protagonists know.

Gaze — The power embedded in looking, theorised by Laura Mulvey as cinema's controlling, usually male, look. Applied to Polanski's watchers, voyeurs and observed women.

Kafkaesque — A predicament in which a helpless individual faces an inscrutable, implacable system. Polanski's version is domestic and social, as in The Tenant.

Long take — An unusually extended shot without a cut. Polanski uses it to let tension accumulate in real time, as on the beach in Cul-de-sac.

Mise-en-scène — Everything placed within the frame and its arrangement: set, light, actors, movement. For Polanski it carries argument, including what the frame withholds.

Neo-noir — Post-classical films that revive film noir's detectives, corruption and fatalism. Chinatown is a founding example.

Nightmare logic — Progression by emotional rather than causal connection, delivered with dreamlike calm. It generates the problem of delusion versus reality.

Pastiche — Imitation of a genre's conventions without parody's mockery. The Polanski genre film enters a form and bends it toward his obsessions.

Subjective camera — Shots aligned with a character's viewpoint, so the viewer sees as they see. Polanski pairs it with amplified sound to externalise a mind.

Trauma theory — The view that overwhelming experience returns belatedly through repetition and displacement. Read into Polanski's enclosures and made explicit in The Pianist.

Uncanny — Freud's term for the familiar made strange, such as a self met outside oneself. It underlies Polanski's apartments, doubles and living walls.
