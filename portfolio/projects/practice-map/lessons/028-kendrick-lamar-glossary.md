# Kendrick Lamar: A Working Glossary of Concepts, Characters, and Ideas, from the Ground Up

## Why a glossary is needed at all

Most rappers can be understood song by song. Kendrick Lamar Duckworth, born June 17, 1987 in Compton, California, cannot. His albums are built as closed systems with recurring characters, internal timelines, acronyms, alter egos, and theological arguments that pay off across records released a decade apart. Listening to him casually is perfectly pleasurable, but listening to him seriously means acquiring a vocabulary. What follows is an attempt to build that vocabulary from the simplest terms up to the interpretive frameworks that scholars, critics, and obsessive fans use, without assuming you already know any of it.

## The basic biographical coordinates

Kendrick grew up in Compton, a small city in south Los Angeles County that N.W.A. made globally synonymous with gang violence in the late 1980s. His parents had moved from Chicago; his father had ties to the Gangster Disciples there. In Compton, Kendrick was raised in a neighborhood with Piru affiliation (Piru being the original Blood set) and attended Centennial High School. He was, by his own account and everyone else's, a quiet, observant kid surrounded by cousins, uncles, and friends who were not quiet. This tension, the watcher inside the chaos, becomes the founding premise of his entire catalog. When he says "good kid" in a "mad city," he means it literally and autobiographically.

He began rapping as **K.Dot**, a teenage battle-rapper name he later shed in favor of his given name, a deliberate move toward personal rather than performed identity. Around 2004 he signed to **Top Dawg Entertainment (TDE)**, an independent Carson-based label run by **Anthony "Top Dawg" Tiffith**, and became one quarter of **Black Hippy**, a loose supergroup with **Jay Rock**, **Ab-Soul**, and **ScHoolboy Q**. In 2011 Dr. Dre, Snoop Dogg, and The Game symbolically passed him the torch of the West Coast on stage, and in 2012 he signed with Dre's Aftermath and Interscope while remaining under TDE. In 2022 he left TDE after fulfilling his contract and now operates through **pgLang**, a creative company he founded in 2020 with his longtime collaborator **Dave Free** (former TDE co-president). pgLang is not a record label in the traditional sense; it describes itself as "a service company" for artists across media, and its opacity is part of the brand.

The other essential name is **Whitney Alford**, his high school sweetheart, fiancée, and mother of his children. She appears on record, most importantly as a voice on *Mr. Morale & the Big Steppers*, and she functions in his work as the fixed point against which his infidelities, addictions, and evasions are measured.

## The early records: Section.80 and the birth of "HiiiPoWeR"

Before the major-label albums came mixtapes (*Training Day*, *C4*, *Overly Dedicated*) and the *Kendrick Lamar EP*. The first work that repays deep study is ***Section.80*** (2011). The title fuses **Section 8**, the federal housing subsidy program, with the **1980s**, the decade of his birth. The album's thesis is generational: children born under Reaganomics and the crack epidemic ("Ronald Reagan Era") inherited conditions they did not create. Two fictional women, **Tammy** and **Keisha**, carry the narrative; "Keisha's Song (Her Pain)" is a portrait of a young sex worker that Kendrick explicitly frames as a response to Tupac's "Brenda's Got a Baby." Already the pattern is visible: real social analysis delivered through composite characters, with a lineage consciously claimed from Tupac Shakur.

The key term from this period is **HiiiPoWeR**, spelled with three i's. Kendrick described it as a movement and mindset: the three i's stand for heart, honor, and respect, and the raised-three-fingers gesture that accompanied it was meant as a self-empowerment symbol rather than a gang sign. It was the first time he articulated a philosophy, and a lot of what came later (the political consciousness of *To Pimp a Butterfly*, the spiritual accounting of *DAMN.*) can be read as HiiiPoWeR maturing and complicating itself.

## good kid, m.A.A.d city: the concept album as short film

***good kid, m.A.A.d city*** (2012) is subtitled "A Short Film by Kendrick Lamar," and the subtitle is a reading instruction. The album is a nonlinear narrative set over roughly one day in Compton around 2004, when Kendrick was seventeen. The listener needs to know a small cast and a few terms.

**m.A.A.d** is an acronym with two official expansions: "my angry adolescence divided" and "my angels on angel dust." The second refers to a specific incident, revealed on the title track, in which young Kendrick unknowingly smoked marijuana laced with PCP, an experience that permanently changed his relationship to drugs and explains why he does not drink or smoke.

**Sherane** is the girl he drives to meet at the album's opening ("Sherane a.k.a. Master Splinter's Daughter"), borrowing his mother's van. The van is a recurring prop; the voicemails from his mother and father asking for it back are the album's comic and moral chorus. Sherane's house turns out to be a setup: two men in black hoodies confront him, and the rest of the album spirals from there through peer pressure ("The Art of Peer Pressure"), a home invasion narrated with disturbing glee ("Backseat Freestyle" is the swaggering fantasy; "The Art of Peer Pressure" is the reality), the economics of the block ("Money Trees," source of the ad-lib **"ya bish"**), and binge drinking ("Swimming Pools (Drank)," a party hit that is actually about alcoholism as inherited behavior).

The album's emotional center is the two-part "Sing About Me, I'm Dying of Thirst." In the first section Kendrick voices two characters: the brother of his murdered friend Dave, and the sister of Keisha from *Section.80*, who is angry at how he portrayed her. Each verse is a request or refusal about being memorialized in song, and Kendrick's own third verse asks whether anyone will sing about him. The second section, after Dave's death in a retaliatory shooting, has the friends encountered by an older woman (voiced by Maya Angelou, according to Kendrick) who leads them through the **Sinner's Prayer**, a moment of baptism and rebirth. "Real," which follows, is the moral resolution, with his parents' voicemails finally delivering the album's thesis: real is responsibility, real is loving yourself. "Compton" with Dr. Dre is the coda, the future that the boy in the van will grow into.

The term critics attach to this record is **survivor's guilt**, and it is important because it never leaves his work. Kendrick got out; Dave did not. Every subsequent album asks what the person who got out owes to the people who did not.

Also from this era is the **"Control" verse** (2013), a guest appearance on a Big Sean song in which Kendrick named a dozen peers (J. Cole, Drake, Big Sean, Pusha T, and others) and declared he was trying to "murder" them lyrically, while calling himself the **King of New York** despite being from Compton. It was understood as a challenge to competitive standards in rap rather than personal beef, but a decade later it looks like the first shot in a war with Drake.

## To Pimp a Butterfly: the poem, the cocoon, and Lucy

***To Pimp a Butterfly*** (2015) is the album most often called a masterpiece and the one most likely to be taught in universities. Its title inverts Harper Lee's *To Kill a Mockingbird*, and its governing metaphor is the caterpillar and the butterfly, explained in full only in the final minutes of the record. The album grew partly out of a trip to South Africa, including Robben Island, where Kendrick stood in Nelson Mandela's cell, and the geography of the record moves between Compton and a wider Black diasporic consciousness.

Musically it is the record where Kendrick fully embraced jazz, funk, and spoken word, working with **Terrace Martin**, **Thundercat**, **Kamasi Washington**, **Robert Glasper**, **Flying Lotus**, **Sounwave**, and George Clinton. Understanding the personnel matters because the album argues that hip-hop is continuous with the entire Black American musical tradition, from Parliament-Funkadelic to bebop to the Last Poets.

The structural device is **the poem**. Beginning with a single line at the end of "King Kunta" ("I remember you was conflicted, misusing your influence"), Kendrick adds lines to a spoken poem across the tracklist. Each track dramatizes the next line. By "Mortal Man" the poem is complete, and he reads it aloud to **Tupac Shakur**, whose voice is lifted from a 1994 interview and spliced into a fictional conversation. Tupac's real words about revolution, poverty, and death are made to answer Kendrick's questions, until Kendrick reads the final metaphor of the caterpillar who is "pimped" by its own environment and the butterfly it becomes, and asks Tupac what he thinks. There is silence. The album ends with the dead man unable to answer, and with Kendrick, the mortal man, left to lead.

Key vocabulary from this album includes **Uncle Sam** ("Wesley's Theory"), who personifies the American economy seducing Black artists into debt and self-destruction (the reference is to Wesley Snipes's tax conviction); **Lucy** ("For Sale? (Interlude)"), a seductive female voice who is Lucifer, offering fame in exchange for the soul; **King Kunta**, fusing Kunta Kinte from *Roots* with the idea of a rapper who became royalty; **"Institutionalized,"** the argument that the ghetto's mental habits follow you to the Grammys; and the paired tracks **"u"** and **"i"**. "u" is a hotel-room breakdown, Kendrick screaming at himself in the second person for abandoning his sister and a dying friend, the most naked self-hatred committed to a major rap record. "i" is its answer, a self-love anthem that on the album is rendered as a live performance interrupted by a fight in the crowd, after which Kendrick delivers an a cappella etymology of the word **"Negus,"** an Ethiopian title meaning king or emperor, as a reclamation of a slur.

"How Much a Dollar Cost" is a parable in which a homeless man asking for a dollar turns out to be God; Barack Obama named it his favorite song of 2015. "The Blacker the Berry" is a furious indictment of anti-Black racism that ends with Kendrick calling himself a hypocrite for mourning Trayvon Martin while having participated in violence against Black men himself, a line that provoked serious debate about respectability politics and internal accountability. And **"Alright,"** with its refrain "we gon' be alright," was chanted at Black Lives Matter protests across the country in 2015 and 2016, making it arguably the most consequential protest song of its decade.

The companion release ***untitled unmastered.*** (2016) collected demos and outtakes from the sessions, identified only by dates; it is the album for people who want to hear the machinery.

## DAMN.: wickedness, weakness, and the reversible tracklist

***DAMN.*** (2017) won the **Pulitzer Prize for Music** in 2018, the first non-classical, non-jazz work ever to do so. The citation praised its "vernacular authenticity and rhythmic dynamism." It was heralded by **"The Heart Part 4,"** in which Kendrick announced "y'all got 'til April 7th," a date that turned out to be one day before the actual release, sending fans into a frenzy of theory.

Every track title is a single word in capital letters followed by a period: BLOOD., DNA., YAH., ELEMENT., FEEL., LOYALTY., PRIDE., HUMBLE., LUST., LOVE., XXX., FEAR., GOD., DUCKWORTH. The album opens with a question: "Is it wickedness? Is it weakness? You decide. Are we gonna live or die?" The dichotomy of **wickedness versus weakness** structures the whole album. The tracks pair off as opposed forces (PRIDE./HUMBLE., LUST./LOVE.), and the album can be understood as a spiritual trial.

The opening track "BLOOD." has Kendrick helping a blind woman and being shot by her, followed by a sample of Fox News hosts (Geraldo Rivera among them) mocking his BET Awards performance of "Alright." Fox News becomes a recurring antagonist; "DNA." explodes in response with a double-time second half that is one of the most technically celebrated verses of his career.

**"FEAR."** is the album's confessional heart, three verses at ages seven, seventeen, and twenty-seven, each cataloguing a different fear: of his mother's beatings, of dying in the streets, of losing everything he has gained. It is framed by voicemails from **Cousin Carl**, a relative who explains that Black suffering is the fulfillment of the curses in **Deuteronomy 28**, a doctrine associated with Hebrew Israelite teaching. Kendrick presents this without full endorsement or rejection, but the theology of a covenant people being punished for disobedience saturates the record, and "YAH." (short for Yahweh) makes the identification explicit: "I'm an Israelite, don't call me Black no more."

The final track, **"DUCKWORTH.,"** tells a true story: decades earlier Anthony Tiffith, the man who would become Top Dawg, nearly robbed and killed a KFC employee named Ducky, who was Kendrick's father. Ducky's kindness (extra chicken, free biscuits) spared him. Had he been killed, Kendrick says, "Top Dawg could be servin' life, while I grew up without a father and die in a gunfight." The track ends with a gunshot and the album rewinding to its first line, and the **Collector's Edition** released later that year presents the entire tracklist in reverse order, confirming that the album is designed to be heard in both directions, with DUCKWORTH. as an alternative beginning and BLOOD. as an alternative end. Which direction you hear it in changes whether the story ends in wickedness or weakness.

The visual persona for this era was **Kung Fu Kenny**, named after Don Cheadle's character in *Rush Hour 2*, and the album's tour and videos were saturated with martial-arts imagery. "HUMBLE." ("sit down, be humble") became his first solo number one, and "LOYALTY." with Rihanna won a Grammy. In 2018 he also curated and largely performed the soundtrack to Marvel's ***Black Panther***, including "All the Stars" with SZA and "King's Dead."

## The five-year silence and "The Heart Part 5"

Between 2018 and 2022 Kendrick released almost nothing under his own name, a gap fans obsessively counted. The most visible activity was mentoring his younger cousin **Baby Keem** and appearing on Keem's "family ties" and "range brothers," which yielded the meme-level phrase "top of the morning." In 2021 he announced through pgLang's website, under a note signed **oklama**, that his next album would be his last for TDE. "Oklama" became a nickname; its meaning has never been clarified, though it echoes Choctaw words and functions as the name of the persona who speaks from within pgLang rather than from the Kendrick of the earlier albums.

**"The Heart Part 5"** arrived in May 2022. The Heart series, dating back to 2010, consists of freeform, non-album songs released before each project as a kind of state-of-the-union. Part 5 is famous for its video, in which deepfake technology morphs Kendrick's face into O.J. Simpson, Kanye West, Jussie Smollett, Will Smith, Kobe Bryant, and Nipsey Hussle, each while the lyrics address themes associated with that man. The song's premise, "I want to represent the culture," is delivered by someone whose face keeps becoming other people, an image about the impossibility of being a singular spokesman.

## Mr. Morale & the Big Steppers: therapy, trauma, and refusing sainthood

***Mr. Morale & the Big Steppers*** (2022) is a double album explicitly structured as therapy. Its recurring sound is the tap dancing of the **Big Steppers**, a term that in slang means people who move on you (violently) but which the album reworks into anyone dancing around their pain. **Mr. Morale** is the title Kendrick gives himself once he emerges from the process: a man trying to live by principle rather than by image. The cover shows him wearing a **crown of thorns** in a domestic scene with Whitney and their children, a gun tucked into his waistband. The German spiritual writer **Eckhart Tolle**, author of *The Power of Now*, appears on the record as a literal voice, and his ideas about ego and the "pain body" are the album's therapeutic framework. Whitney, too, speaks, at one point telling him to "tell them the truth."

The opening line, "I've been going through something / 1,855 days," counts the exact gap since *DAMN.* The album then proceeds through a catalogue of what he calls generational curses. "Father Time" (with Sampha) examines what he calls "daddy issues," the way a hard father produces a son who cannot show vulnerability. "We Cry Together" is a scripted argument with actress Taylour Paige, a Marvin Gaye–style domestic fight that becomes unbearable by design. "Auntie Diaries" tells of a transgender uncle and a transgender cousin, and it became the album's most debated track because Kendrick uses deadnames and the f-slur in the process of describing his own evolution out of prejudice; whether the song is a model of honest growth or a clumsy imposition on trans people's experience remains argued. "Mother I Sober" (with Portishead's Beth Gibbons) is the culmination: Kendrick recounts his mother's belief that he had been molested as a child, his insistence that he had not, and the way inherited sexual trauma in his family shaped his own **lust addiction** and infidelity. The track ends with Whitney's voice announcing "you broke a generational curse."

The other crucial concept is the refusal of the **savior** role. "Savior" states flatly: "Kendrick made you think about it, but he is not your savior." Having been made an oracle by the reception of *To Pimp a Butterfly* and the Pulitzer, he uses this album to decline the position, to the frustration of listeners who wanted political statements during the pandemic and the 2020 protests. His decision to feature **Kodak Black**, a rapper who pleaded guilty to assault charges involving a minor, was part of this gesture: Kodak is presented as a mirror, a man from the same conditions who did not get out, and Kendrick's willingness to associate with him rather than condemn him was itself a rejection of the moral spokesman role. "Mirror" closes the album with the line "I choose me, I'm sorry," which is either a healthy act of boundary-setting or a self-absorbed abdication depending on who is listening.

## The Drake war of 2024

To understand Kendrick's present cultural standing, you need the sequence of the 2024 feud with Drake, which was the largest rap beef since Jay-Z and Nas and arguably the most one-sided in history.

The precipitating event was "First Person Shooter" (2023), a Drake and J. Cole song in which Cole called himself, Drake, and Kendrick the "big three." In March 2024 Kendrick appeared on Future and Metro Boomin's **"Like That"** with the rejoinder "motherfuck the big three, it's just big me." Drake responded with "Push Ups" and then "Taylor Made Freestyle," which used AI-generated voices of Tupac and Snoop Dogg to taunt Kendrick; Tupac's estate threatened suit and the song was pulled. Kendrick then released **"euphoria"** (a six-minute dissection of Drake's authenticity, race, and parenting), **"6:16 in LA"** (suggesting he had a mole inside Drake's camp), and, within about a day of Drake's "Family Matters," the pair of **"meet the grahams"** and **"Not Like Us."** "Meet the grahams" is addressed letter by letter to Drake's son, his parents, and an alleged hidden daughter, and it is as cold a record as exists in the genre. "Not Like Us," produced by **Mustard**, is its opposite: a bouncing West Coast club anthem built on a single unforgettable accusation ("certified lover boy? certified pedophile") and the pun on **"A minor"** that doubles as musical notation and a reference to underage girls. The title phrase, **"they not like us,"** framed Drake as a Canadian cultural tourist in Black American music, and the chorus's "OV-hoe" (mocking Drake's OVO brand) became a schoolyard chant. Drake's response, "The Heart Part 6," stealing Kendrick's series title, was widely judged a concession.

The war then moved into spectacle. On June 19, 2024 (Juneteenth) Kendrick staged **The Pop Out: Ken & Friends** at the Kia Forum in Inglewood, performing "Not Like Us" five times in a row and bringing rival Bloods and Crips onstage together, an image of West Coast unity that turned a diss track into a civic event. In February 2025 he headlined the **Super Bowl LIX halftime show**, with Samuel L. Jackson playing Uncle Sam (the character from "Wesley's Theory") as a scolding narrator, Serena Williams crip-walking during "Not Like Us," and the stage arranged as a PlayStation controller and a prison yard, ending with the words "GAME OVER" lit up in the crowd. Earlier that month "Not Like Us" won five Grammys, including Record of the Year and Song of the Year. Drake, for his part, sued Universal Music Group for defamation over the song's promotion. Whatever the legal outcome, the phrase **"Not Like Us"** now functions in American culture as shorthand for a total public victory.

## GNX: the victory lap and the "reincarnated" thesis

***GNX*** (November 2024) was released with no warning during the height of the feud. The title refers to the **Buick Grand National Experimental**, a black 1987 muscle car (the year of his birth) that appears on the cover; his father brought him home from the hospital in a Buick Regal. Produced largely by Sounwave, **Jack Antonoff**, and Mustard, the album is shorter and more direct than anything since *good kid*, and it is stuffed with vocabulary that became instantly memetic: **"squabble up"** (a West Coast term for fighting, built on a Debbie Deb freestyle sample), the shouted **"MUSTAAAARD"** from "tv off," and **"wacced out murals,"** the opener, which responds to the vandalism of a Compton mural of Kendrick and airs grievances with Lil Wayne and Snoop over the Super Bowl selection.

Conceptually, the key track is **"reincarnated,"** built on a Tupac "Made N****z" beat, in which Kendrick imagines his previous lives as a blues guitarist and a jazz-era singer destroyed by addiction, then dialogues with God about whether his talent has been used for pride or for service. It reopens the theological argument of *DAMN.* and answers it: the gift was given to be given away. "luther" with SZA, built on a Luther Vandross and Cheryl Lynn sample, became a lasting hit, and "heart pt. 6," reclaiming the title Drake stole, tells the origin story of TDE and his split from it with affection rather than bitterness. "man at the garden" repeats "I deserve it all," a phrase that, read against the self-flagellation of "u," shows how far the psychological arc has traveled. The 2025 **Grand National Tour** with SZA followed.

## Cross-cutting interpretive concepts

Having assembled the parts, it helps to name the throughlines that connect them, since these are the ideas serious analysis returns to.

**The divided self.** From the "m.A.A.d" acronym ("my angry adolescence divided") through "u" and "i," through "wickedness or weakness," through Mr. Morale and the Big Steppers, Kendrick consistently imagines himself as two. His characteristic technique of pitch-shifting his voice into different registers within a single song, sometimes within a single verse, is the sonic expression of this. He does not rap in one voice because he does not believe he is one person.

**Survivor's guilt and the ethics of escape.** Every album asks what the one who left owes to Compton, to Dave, to Keisha, to Kodak Black. "Momma" and "Sing About Me" pose it; "Savior" and "Mirror" attempt to set a limit on it.

**Temptation personified.** Uncle Sam, Lucy, and the Israelite curses form a continuous theology of seduction and covenant. Kendrick is a religious writer in the tradition of Augustine or Bunyan more than of most rappers; his sins are catalogued not to shock but because he believes they are being counted.

**Storytelling architecture.** His albums use skits, voicemails, poems, reversed sequencing, and framed narratives, and they reward being heard in order. The "short film" of *good kid*, the accumulating poem of *TPAB*, the palindrome of *DAMN.*, and the therapy session of *Mr. Morale* are four different solutions to the same problem: how to make forty minutes of rap behave like a novel.

**The West Coast lineage.** Tupac is the ghost in every room. The interview on "Mortal Man," the Tupac beat on "reincarnated," the AI Tupac that Drake fatally misjudged, and the Super Bowl staged in the shadow of Dre's own halftime show all locate Kendrick as the inheritor of a specific California tradition. The Compton geography (Rosecrans Avenue, the Piru sets, Centennial High, the van) is not local color; it is the source of his authority.

**The refusal and reclamation of the spokesman role.** *TPAB* made him a political oracle; *Mr. Morale* renounced the position; "Not Like Us" and the Super Bowl accepted it again on his own terms, as a West Coast unifier rather than a national conscience. The story of his career is partly the story of a man negotiating how much of the culture's weight he is willing to carry.

## A minimal working vocabulary, for quick reference

If a reader retains nothing else, these are the terms that unlock conversation with any Kendrick enthusiast: **K.Dot** (the early name), **TDE** and **Top Dawg** (the label and its founder), **Black Hippy** (the crew), **pgLang** and **Dave Free** (the current company and partner), **HiiiPoWeR** (the first philosophy), **Section.80** (housing plus decade), **m.A.A.d** (the acronym), **Sherane** and the **van** (the *good kid* setup), **"ya bish,"** **Dave** and the **Sinner's Prayer** (the *good kid* climax), **the poem** and **the caterpillar and butterfly** (the *TPAB* frame), **Uncle Sam** and **Lucy** (the tempters), **"u"/"i,"** **Negus,** **"Alright"** (the anthem), **Mortal Man** and the **Tupac interview**, **wickedness or weakness**, **Cousin Carl** and **Deuteronomy 28**, **FEAR.**, **DUCKWORTH.** and the **reversible tracklist**, **Kung Fu Kenny**, the **Pulitzer**, **oklama**, **The Heart series**, **Mr. Morale**, **Big Steppers** and **tap dancing**, **Eckhart Tolle**, **Whitney**, **the crown of thorns**, **generational curse**, **"not your savior,"** **"I choose me,"** **Baby Keem**, **"Like That"** and **"big me,"** **euphoria**, **meet the grahams**, **Not Like Us**, **"A minor,"** **The Pop Out**, **Super Bowl LIX**, **GNX** (the Buick), **"squabble up,"** **"MUSTARD,"** **reincarnated**, and **"I deserve it all."**

Learn those and the conversation opens. Understand how they connect and you begin to see why a rapper from Compton is discussed in the same breath as novelists and theologians, and why the man who once asked whether anyone would sing about him has spent the last fifteen years making sure the answer is yes.
