# Discworld: A World Made of Story

## An Introductory Lesson for Curious Minds

---

## Prologue: What You Are Looking At

Between 1983 and 2015, the British novelist Sir Terry Pratchett published forty-one novels set on the Discworld, a flat planet carried through space on the backs of four elephants, who in turn stand on the shell of an enormous turtle. It began as parody. In *The Colour of Magic*, Pratchett was lampooning the swords-and-sorcery clichés of Tolkien imitators, Robert E. Howard, and Fritz Leiber. Over three decades it became one of the most sophisticated bodies of satirical fiction in the English language. It is a sustained inquiry into belief, power, narrative, justice, and what it means to be human, and it happens to be very funny.

The single most useful idea for approaching Discworld is this: **it is a world in which metaphors are load-bearing.** On our world, we say that "stories shape reality," that "gods live only as long as people believe in them," or that "Death comes for us all," and we mean these things figuratively. On the Disc, they are literally, physically, mechanically true. Pratchett's method is to take an abstraction, make it concrete, and then follow the consequences with rigorous logic. The comedy comes from the literalization. The insight comes from the logic.

This lesson walks through the core concepts in roughly the order a new reader meets them, moving from cosmology to metaphysics to politics to ethics.

---

## I. The Cosmology: A Joke With Engineering Behind It

The Disc itself is borrowed from real mythology. The image of the world resting on elephants and a turtle appears in Hindu cosmological traditions and was popularized in the West through anecdotes about the infinite regress of explanation ("it's turtles all the way down"). Pratchett's innovation was to treat this image not as a naive primitive belief to be laughed at, but as an engineering specification.

The world-turtle is called **Great A'Tuin**. The four elephants are **Berilia, Tubul, Great T'Phon, and Jerakeen**. A'Tuin swims slowly through space toward an unknown destination, and the Disc's astronomers and philosophers argue about where it is going and about its sex. Some favor the "Big Bang" hypothesis, in which the turtle is swimming toward a mating encounter. Others favor the "Steady Gait" hypothesis, in which it just keeps swimming forever. This is Pratchett gently parodying real twentieth-century debates in cosmology. It is also a small lesson in how scientific communities argue from limited data.

Because the world is flat and circular, the compass is different. You do not go north or south. You go **Hubward**, toward the center, where a vast mountain called Cori Celesti hosts the gods, or **Rimward**, toward the edge, where the oceans pour endlessly off into space in a waterfall called the Rimfall. The two directions of rotation are **Turnwise** and **Widdershins**. A small sun and moon orbit the Disc, and the seasons depend on where the sun is in its orbit relative to the world. Occasionally an elephant has to lift a leg to let the sun pass.

Two details matter for everything that follows:

1. **The Disc is saturated with magic.** Its magical field is so strong that light moves sluggishly through it. There is also an eighth color in the spectrum, **octarine**, the color of magic itself, described as a kind of fluorescent greenish-yellow purple. It is visible to wizards and cats.

2. **The world should not physically work.** A flat disc on elephants violates physics. It exists because the universe on the Disc runs on different fundamental principles, and that brings us to the most important concept in the series.

## II. Narrativium: The Element of Story

In *The Science of Discworld* (1999), co-written with mathematician Ian Stewart and biologist Jack Cohen, Pratchett gave a name to the principle that had quietly governed the Disc from the beginning: **narrativium**, the fundamental element of story.

On our world, the basic building blocks are particles and forces. Things happen because of physical causation. On the Disc, there is an additional ingredient. Things happen because *that is how the story goes*. This is called **narrative causality**. Once a story has started, it wants to reach its proper ending, and it will bend events and people to get there.

The most famous expression of this is a running joke. On the Disc, "million-to-one chances crop up nine times out of ten." A desperate hero taking a one-in-a-million shot at a dragon will almost certainly succeed, because that is what happens in stories. In *Guards! Guards!*, characters try to calculate whether their odds are *exactly* a million to one. If they are slightly better, the narrative magic won't apply, so they try to make their situation worse.

This is hilarious, but it is also a serious philosophical move. Pratchett is making a claim about *us*. Human beings do not experience the world as physics. We experience it as narrative. We see heroes and villains, rising action, fitting ends, and poetic justice, and we act accordingly. The Disc makes this cognitive habit into a law of nature. That lets Pratchett explore both its power and its danger. Stories can give people courage and meaning. They can also trap people in roles they never chose: the wicked stepmother, the doomed younger son, the princess who must be rescued.

Many of Pratchett's best characters are people who *notice* the story and refuse to play along. The witch Granny Weatherwax, in *Witches Abroad*, fights against a fairy godmother who is forcing an entire city into the shape of a fairytale, whether its inhabitants like it or not. The antagonist is not a monster. It is **narrative itself, used as a tool of control.**

## III. Belief as a Physical Force

If stories shape reality, then so does belief, which is the fuel stories run on. On the Disc, belief has measurable consequences.

**Gods** are the clearest case. In *Small Gods* (1992), often cited as the best standalone entry point into the series, we learn that Discworld gods begin as tiny, voiceless spirits. They grow in power according to how many people believe in them. The great god Om, whose church dominates a theocratic nation, discovers that he has shrunk into the body of a small tortoise. His vast religious institution is full of people who believe in the *church*, its rituals, its hierarchy, and its power to punish, but only one person, a simple novice named Brutha, still believes in *Om*. The novel is a brilliant dissection of the difference between faith and institutional religion. It asks what happens when an apparatus of belief outlives the belief that justified it.

The same principle creates **anthropomorphic personifications**: abstract concepts that take on physical form because humanity has imagined them so persistently. The most beloved of these is **Death**.

Death on the Disc is a seven-foot skeleton in a black robe who carries a scythe, rides a pale horse named Binky, and speaks IN SMALL CAPITAL LETTERS WITHOUT QUOTATION MARKS. He is not malevolent. He is a professional who does a necessary job, and over the course of the series he becomes fascinated by humanity. He likes cats and curry. He tries, with mixed success, to understand fun. In *Mort* he takes on an apprentice. In *Reaper Man* he is forced into retirement and experiences mortality for himself. He becomes, paradoxically, one of the most compassionate characters in the entire series.

The deepest statement of the belief theme comes in *Hogfather* (1996), the Disc's version of the Santa Claus myth. Death explains to his granddaughter Susan why it matters that children believe in small fictions:

> "Humans need fantasy to be *human*. To be the place where the falling angel meets the rising ape."

Justice, mercy, and duty, he points out, do not exist as physical things. You cannot grind the universe into powder and find one atom of justice. Yet humans act as though these ideals were real, and by doing so, they make them real. Children who practice believing in the small lies grow into adults who can believe in the big ones. This is Pratchett's humanism at its most concentrated. Our moral ideals are fictions, and they are the most important fictions we have.

The opposite of this view is embodied by the **Auditors of Reality**: grey, faceless cosmic bureaucrats who want a universe of pure, predictable, lifeless order. They despise individuality, because anything with a name and a personality is messy and unaccountable. They recur as the closest thing the series has to metaphysical villains. What they threaten is the reduction of all meaning to measurement.

## IV. Magic: Two Philosophies of Power

Magic on the Disc splits into two traditions, and the contrast between them is one of the series' richest ideas.

**Wizards** are academic, male (at least in the traditional institution), and headquartered at **Unseen University** in the city of Ankh-Morpork. Magic here is powerful, measurable, and extremely dangerous. Pratchett even provides a unit: the **thaum**, defined as the amount of magic required to create one small white pigeon or three normal-sized billiard balls. Too much concentrated magic thins the walls of reality and attracts the **Things from the Dungeon Dimensions**, Lovecraftian horrors who want in.

As a result, the wisest wizards have concluded that the best use of magic is almost never to use it. Unseen University is, in effect, a place where dangerous men are kept comfortable, well-fed, and busy with committee meetings so that they do not accidentally end the world. It is a satire of academia that also contains a real argument about restraint in the face of overwhelming power. Readers in the nuclear age will recognize the logic. Notable residents include the bluff, hearty Archchancellor Mustrum Ridcully; the cowardly, perpetually fleeing Rincewind, protagonist of the earliest books; and the **Librarian**, who was turned into an orangutan by a magical accident and refuses to be turned back. He communicates almost entirely in the word "Ook."

The Librarian introduces a delightful geek-friendly concept: **L-space**. Books contain knowledge, knowledge is power, power is energy, energy is matter, and matter has mass, which distorts space. Therefore a sufficiently large accumulation of books bends space-time. All libraries, everywhere and everywhen, are connected through L-space, and trained librarians can travel between them. It is a joke built on a deliberately bad syllogism that nonetheless captures something true about how libraries feel.

**Witches**, by contrast, are rural, practical, mostly female, and deeply suspicious of flashy spells. Their greatest representative is **Granny Esmerelda Weatherwax** of the mountain kingdom of Lancre, perhaps Pratchett's single finest character. Granny can do real magic. She can "Borrow" the minds of animals, for instance. But most of her power comes from **headology**: a sophisticated understanding of human psychology, expectation, and reputation. If someone believes a witch has cured them, they often get better. If a bully believes a witch can see straight through him, he usually backs down.

Witchcraft is presented as the work of standing at boundaries: between life and death, village and wilderness, and what people want and what they need. Witches deliver babies, sit with the dying, and make the hard decisions nobody else wants to make. In the later *Tiffany Aching* novels, written for younger readers but every bit as profound, a young witch learns to cultivate **"First Sight and Second Thoughts."** First Sight means seeing what is *actually there* rather than what you expect. Second Thoughts means watching your own thinking critically. It is essentially a manual for clear, honest thought.

The moral core of the witches, and arguably of the entire series, comes from Granny Weatherwax in *Carpe Jugulum*:

> "Sin, young man, is when you treat people as things. Including yourself. That's what sin is."

## V. Ankh-Morpork: The Engine of Modernity

If the early Discworld was about fantasy, the mature Discworld is about **the city**, specifically the vast, filthy, magnificent metropolis of **Ankh-Morpork**, a sort of composite of medieval London, Victorian London, and every city that ever ran on commerce and corruption.

Ankh-Morpork is ruled by **Lord Havelock Vetinari**, the Patrician, a tyrant whose genius lies in understanding that the city is a self-regulating system. He does not suppress crime. He licenses it. The **Thieves' Guild** is permitted a fixed annual quota of theft, and in return it ruthlessly punishes unlicensed criminals, making the city orderly by making disorder a regulated profession. Vetinari believes in "one man, one vote": he is the man, and he has the vote. He is terrifying, and he is also very nearly the best ruler the city could possibly have. That uncomfortable ambiguity is precisely the point. Pratchett uses him to explore the difference between legitimacy and competence.

Opposite Vetinari stands **Sam Vimes**, commander of the City Watch, a cynical, working-class policeman whose rage against injustice drives the *Watch* novels. Vimes is the source of one of Pratchett's most widely quoted ideas, the **"Boots" Theory of Socioeconomic Unfairness** from *Men at Arms*. A rich man buys a pair of good boots for fifty dollars that last ten years. A poor man can only afford ten-dollar boots, which wear out in a season. Over ten years, the poor man spends far more money and still has wet feet. Poverty, in other words, is expensive. This is a real economic insight, cited today by economists and journalists, and delivered in a fantasy novel.

Across the later books, Ankh-Morpork undergoes its own **industrial and information revolution**. A newspaper appears in *The Truth*. A national semaphore network called **the clacks**, essentially a mechanical internet, emerges before *Going Postal*. Paper money arrives in *Making Money*, and a railway in *Raising Steam*. Pratchett uses these books to examine how technology changes power, labor, truth, and community.

The clacks give us a moving piece of real-world geek culture. In *Going Postal*, clacks operators honor a dead colleague by keeping his name perpetually moving through the network with the code "GNU": *send it on, do not log it, turn it around at the end of the line.* As Pratchett puts it, "a man is not dead while his name is still spoken." After Pratchett died in 2015, web developers began inserting the HTTP header `X-Clacks-Overhead: GNU Terry Pratchett` into their servers. It is still there, on countless sites, keeping his name in the overhead.

## VI. Why It Matters: Pratchett's Method

Stepping back, several features explain why Discworld rewards serious readers:

**It is satire in the classical sense.** Like Swift or Voltaire, Pratchett uses an invented world to see our own world more clearly. Its targets include war (*Jingo*), religious fundamentalism (*Small Gods*), racism and assimilation (the long arc of dwarves, trolls, and goblins in the Watch books), gender roles (*Equal Rites*, *Monstrous Regiment*), and the police state (*Night Watch*).

**It is humanist without being naive.** Pratchett's characters are often petty, foolish, and cruel. His anger at injustice is palpable. Colleague Neil Gaiman famously described the fury that fueled Pratchett's writing. Yet the books insist that ordinary decency, practiced stubbornly, matters more than grand destinies.

**It is formally playful.** The famous footnotes, the typographical voice of Death, the elaborate running jokes, and the parodies of everything from *Macbeth* to *Phantom of the Opera* to Hollywood reward careful, allusion-hungry reading.

**It develops.** The early books are loose, parodic, and a bit rough. By the middle of the series, Pratchett writes tightly plotted novels with genuine emotional weight. Watching the world deepen is part of the pleasure.

## VII. Where to Begin

The novels can be read in publication order, but most readers enter through one of several sub-series, each with its own flavor:

- **For the concepts discussed here,** try *Small Gods*, a standalone that needs no prior knowledge.
- **For police procedural and political satire,** start the City Watch with *Guards! Guards!*
- **For the philosophy of mortality,** start the Death books with *Mort*.
- **For folklore, psychology, and power,** start the Witches with *Wyrd Sisters*, or begin with *The Wee Free Men*, the first Tiffany Aching book.
- **For technology and economics,** try *Going Postal*.

Readers are generally advised not to judge the series by its first two books. *The Colour of Magic* and *The Light Fantastic* are charming, but the Disc had not yet found its voice.

## Epilogue: The Turtle Moves

In *Small Gods*, a persecuted philosopher defies the Church's teaching, which, like Galileo's inquisitors, insists on a false cosmology. His rallying cry is simply: *"The Turtle Moves."* It is the Disc's version of *Eppur si muove*, "and yet it moves." It is a statement that truth is what it is, regardless of what the powerful insist.

That phrase is a fitting summary of Discworld itself. Underneath the jokes, the elephants, and the orangutan librarian is a body of work committed to looking clearly at the world, telling the truth about it, and defending the fragile human fictions that make truth worth telling.
