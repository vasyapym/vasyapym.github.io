# Uzbekistan: A Conceptual Lesson

## Why This Country Is Worth Understanding

Uzbekistan is the demographic and cultural heart of Central Asia, home to roughly 37 million people, more than the other four post-Soviet Central Asian states combined. It holds Samarkand and Bukhara, two of the most storied cities in human history. It is one of only two countries on Earth that are *doubly landlocked*, meaning you must cross at least two borders to reach an ocean. It was the site of one of the twentieth century's worst environmental catastrophes. And since 2016 it has been running one of the more interesting experiments in post-authoritarian economic opening anywhere in the world. If you want to understand how empires, ideologies, ecology, and geography stack on top of one another to produce a modern state, Uzbekistan is an unusually rich case study.

What follows is organized around concepts rather than chronology, though history threads through all of it. The goal is that a complete beginner can follow every paragraph while a specialist finds the framing rigorous enough to argue with.

## Concept One: Geography as Destiny, Then as Trap

Start with water, because in Central Asia everything starts with water. The region is dominated by arid steppe and desert; the Kyzylkum ("red sand") occupies much of Uzbekistan's interior. Life clusters along two great rivers, the Amu Darya (the Oxus of Greek geographers) and the Syr Darya (the Jaxartes), both fed by glacial melt from the Pamir and Tian Shan mountains to the east. Between and around these rivers lie the historical oases: Khorezm in the northwest delta, the Zeravshan valley with Samarkand and Bukhara, and the Fergana Valley in the east, a densely populated bowl of astonishing agricultural fertility.

Historians use the term *Transoxiana* ("beyond the Oxus") or its Arabic equivalent *Mawarannahr* for this zone, and the term matters because it captures a structural fact: this was a land of sedentary, irrigated, urban civilization ringed by nomadic pastoralists. The interplay between oasis and steppe, between farmer and herder, between city-dweller and horseman, is the deep engine of the region's history. Cities produced wealth, craft, scholarship, and religion; the steppe produced military power and periodically conquered the cities, after which the conquerors settled, assimilated, and became the next patrons of urban culture. This cycle repeated for two millennia.

Geography made the region central in the literal sense. Overland routes between China, India, Persia, and the Mediterranean funneled through these oases, and the term "Silk Road" (coined only in the nineteenth century by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen) is shorthand for this connectivity. Being central meant being wealthy and cosmopolitan. But the same geography that made Uzbekistan central when trade moved by camel made it peripheral when trade moved by ship. After roughly 1500, as European maritime routes displaced the overland caravans, Central Asia slowly slid from the center of the Eurasian economy to its margins. The doubly-landlocked status that is a trivia fact today was, historically, the difference between prosperity and isolation. Much of contemporary Uzbek strategy is an attempt to reverse that slide by making land connectivity valuable again.

## Concept Two: The Layered Civilizational Inheritance

To understand the Uzbek sense of self, you need to see the strata.

The oldest layer is Iranian. The ancient inhabitants, Sogdians and Bactrians and Khorezmians, spoke Eastern Iranian languages, practiced Zoroastrianism and later Buddhism and Manichaeism, and ran the Silk Road as its merchant class. Sogdian was the lingua franca of Central Asian trade for centuries. This is why Bukhara and Samarkand remain, to this day, substantially Tajik-speaking cities, and why the Persianate cultural template of poetry, garden design, and courtly manners runs so deep.

The second layer is Islamic and Arab, arriving with the conquests of the eighth century. The decisive battle was Talas in 751, where Abbasid forces defeated a Tang Chinese army and settled the question of whether Central Asia would face west toward Islam or east toward China. Under the Samanid dynasty (ninth and tenth centuries), Bukhara became one of the intellectual capitals of the Islamic world. This is the era that produced al-Khwarizmi, whose name gives us the word "algorithm" and whose book title gives us "algebra"; al-Biruni, a polymath of staggering range; Ibn Sina (Avicenna), whose *Canon of Medicine* was a European medical textbook into the seventeenth century; and al-Bukhari, compiler of the most authoritative collection of hadith in Sunni Islam. When Uzbeks today speak of a golden age, this is often what they mean. It is worth noting that this golden age was Persian-speaking and predates the arrival of the Uzbeks as a people.

The third layer is Turkic. Turkic-speaking nomads had been pressing into the region for centuries, but the Karakhanids in the tenth and eleventh centuries were the first Turkic Muslim dynasty to rule the oases. Turkic gradually became the language of the countryside and the military while Persian remained the language of administration and high culture. A bilingual, bicultural society emerged in which the same person might speak Turkic at home and compose poetry in Persian. This Turco-Persian synthesis is the single most important thing to grasp about Central Asian identity.

The fourth layer is Mongol. Genghis Khan's armies devastated the region in 1220, sacking Bukhara and Samarkand with a thoroughness that became legendary. But the Mongol aftermath is more interesting than the conquest. From the wreckage arose Timur (Tamerlane) in the late fourteenth century, a Turkicized Mongol warlord from near Samarkand who built an empire stretching from Delhi to Ankara and made Samarkand his showcase capital. The Timurid period is the source of Uzbekistan's most famous architecture: the turquoise domes and monumental portals of the Registan, the Gur-e-Amir mausoleum, the Bibi-Khanym mosque. Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg built an observatory whose star catalog was the most accurate in the world before the telescope. The Timurid court in Herat and Samarkand produced Ali-Shir Nava'i, who deliberately elevated Chagatai Turkic into a literary language to rival Persian, and who is today celebrated as the founder of Uzbek literature.

The fifth layer, and the one that gives the country its name, is the Uzbek proper. Around 1500, a confederation of Turco-Mongol nomads from the northern steppe, led by Muhammad Shaybani and calling themselves Uzbeks after an earlier khan of the Golden Horde, swept south and overthrew the Timurids. The last Timurid prince, Babur, fled to India and founded the Mughal Empire, which is why the Taj Mahal has architectural cousins in Samarkand. The Shaybanids and their successors ruled Transoxiana through three khanates, Bukhara, Khiva, and Kokand, that persisted until the Russian conquest.

Here is the conceptual punchline: modern Uzbek national identity claims Timur as its founding hero, even though the historical Uzbeks were precisely the people who destroyed Timur's dynasty. This is not hypocrisy so much as a normal feature of nation-building, in which a modern state reaches back into a territorial past and claims whatever is glorious there. Every nation does this. Uzbekistan simply does it with unusual visibility, and understanding the gap between the territorial past and the ethnic past is the beginning of thinking clearly about the country.

## Concept Three: Colonialism, Russian Style

The Russian Empire conquered the region between roughly 1865 and 1885, taking Tashkent in 1865, reducing Bukhara and Khiva to protectorates, and annexing Kokand outright. The motives combined strategic rivalry with Britain (the so-called Great Game), commercial interest in cotton after the American Civil War disrupted supply, and the generic imperial appetite of the era.

Russian rule introduced patterns that persist. Tashkent, previously a secondary town, became the administrative capital and grew into a dual city with a European quarter laid out on a grid beside the old Islamic city. Railways arrived. Most consequentially, the Russians began the systematic expansion of cotton cultivation, laying the groundwork for the monoculture that would later define and deform the Soviet economy of the region.

Intellectually, the late imperial period produced the Jadids, a movement of Muslim modernist reformers who wanted to reconcile Islam with modern education, science, and eventually nationalism. They founded new-method schools, published newspapers, and debated what a modern Turkestani identity should look like. The Jadids matter because they represent an indigenous path to modernity that was foreclosed, first by Tsarist suspicion and then, more brutally, by Stalin, who had most of them shot in the 1930s. Post-independence Uzbekistan has rehabilitated them as national heroes, which tells you something about the state's preferred genealogy for itself: modern, Muslim but secular-leaning, and distinct from both Russian communism and Islamic radicalism.

## Concept Four: The Soviet Manufacture of a Nation

This is the most conceptually demanding section and arguably the most important.

Before the 1920s, there was no "Uzbekistan" and, in a meaningful sense, no unified "Uzbek" people. Inhabitants of the region identified by religion (Muslim), by locality (a Bukharan, a Khivan, a Ferganan), by lifestyle (settled *Sart* versus nomadic), by tribe, or by language in a loose and situational way. The bilingual Turco-Persian world did not sort people neatly into ethnic boxes.

The Soviets, applying Stalin's theory of nationality, decided it must. In 1924 the *national delimitation* carved the old Turkestan into republics defined by ethnolinguistic nations: Uzbek, Turkmen, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and, initially as an autonomous region within Uzbekistan, Tajik. Ethnographers and linguists were dispatched to determine who was what. Boundaries were drawn, in some places sensibly, in others in ways that seem designed to prevent any republic from being viable alone. The Fergana Valley was split three ways among Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, and Tajikistan, creating a jigsaw of enclaves and exclaves that generates border friction to this day. Samarkand and Bukhara, largely Persian-speaking, went to Uzbekistan rather than Tajikistan, a grievance Tajik nationalists still nurse.

Then the Soviets built the nation they had declared. They standardized a literary Uzbek language, choosing a dialect base, switching the script from Arabic to Latin in the late 1920s and then to Cyrillic in 1940. They wrote national histories, founded national academies, staged national operas, and composed a canon. The theory was that national form would carry socialist content, and that once modernized, nations would eventually merge into a single Soviet people. The practice was that they created durable national identities that outlived the Soviet Union itself.

Scholars call this the paradox of Soviet nationality policy: an ideology committed in principle to the withering away of nations became history's most prolific manufacturer of them. Uzbekistan is perhaps the clearest example. By 1991, the Uzbek identity that the Soviets had assembled from Turco-Persian raw materials was solid enough to serve as the foundation of an independent state, and the elites the Soviets had trained to run the republic simply carried on running it.

The Soviet period also delivered mass literacy, universal education, a public health system, industrialization, the emancipation (in law and substantially in fact) of women, and the physical infrastructure of a modern country. It delivered these alongside collectivization famine, the purges, forced secularization that closed nearly all mosques and madrasas, and an economic role as raw-material periphery. Any honest assessment holds both ledgers open at once.

## Concept Five: Cotton, the Aral Sea, and the Political Ecology of Monoculture

If you understand one thing about Uzbekistan's economy, understand cotton.

Moscow assigned Uzbekistan the role of cotton supplier to the Soviet textile industry, and the republic was reorganized around that role. Enormous irrigation networks, including the Karakum Canal in Turkmenistan and vast systems in Uzbekistan, diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya into cotton fields. Cotton acreage expanded relentlessly; by the 1980s, Uzbekistan produced two-thirds of Soviet cotton and was the world's second-largest exporter. The crop was called "white gold." Schoolchildren, students, teachers, doctors, and office workers were mobilized every autumn for the harvest.

The consequences were catastrophic on three fronts.

Ecologically, the rivers stopped reaching the Aral Sea. Once the fourth-largest lake on Earth, it lost roughly ninety percent of its volume between 1960 and the 2000s. The fishing town of Moynaq is now dozens of kilometers from water, its boats rusting in sand. The exposed seabed, laced with salt and agricultural chemicals, blows across the region as toxic dust, and the surrounding Karakalpakstan region suffers elevated rates of respiratory disease, cancers, and infant mortality. The Aral Sea is the standard textbook case of anthropogenic environmental collapse, and it is not an accident but a policy outcome: the planners knew, and judged the cotton worth it.

Economically, monoculture starved the republic of diversification and locked in a dependency that persisted after independence. Cotton was a state monopoly; farmers were assigned quotas and paid below world prices, with the difference captured by the state. This was the fiscal spine of the post-Soviet regime.

Politically, cotton produced a distinctive pathology. The "cotton scandal" of the 1980s, in which Uzbek officials had for years reported fictitious harvests to Moscow and pocketed payments for cotton that did not exist, became the largest corruption case in Soviet history and was used by Gorbachev's reformers to purge the Uzbek party. The episode humiliated Uzbek elites and fed a nationalist resentment that shaped the independence era. And the forced mobilization of labor for the harvest continued, under independent Uzbekistan, into the 2010s, drawing international boycotts of Uzbek cotton. Its abolition, confirmed by the International Labour Organization around 2021, is one of the concrete achievements of the reform period.

The lesson, generalizable beyond Uzbekistan, is that resource monocultures shape not just economies but institutions, ecologies, and the moral texture of governance. Cotton did to Uzbekistan something structurally similar to what oil does to petrostates.

## Concept Six: The Karimov System

Uzbekistan did not so much win independence as have it thrust upon it when the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. Islam Karimov, the republic's Communist Party first secretary, became president and remained so until his death in 2016.

The Karimov system can be understood as an answer to a question he asked explicitly: how does a new state with contested borders, a fragile economy, and a potentially restive Muslim population avoid becoming Tajikistan, which collapsed into civil war in 1992, or Afghanistan? His answer was stability above all, achieved through a strong security apparatus, a tightly controlled political space, and economic autarky.

The security dimension was severe. Independent political parties were suppressed, media was controlled, and the National Security Service surveilled everyone. Islam was the particular target. Karimov distinguished between "traditional" Islam, which the state would sponsor through an official religious directorate, and "extremism," a category elastic enough to include anyone who prayed too visibly or attended the wrong mosque. The Islamic Movement of Uzbekistan, a genuinely militant group, provided the pretext; the crackdown swept far wider. Thousands were imprisoned. The nadir was Andijan in May 2005, when security forces fired on a crowd of protesters and killed several hundred people. Western criticism led Karimov to evict the United States from the Karshi-Khanabad airbase it had used for Afghan operations and to pivot toward Russia and China. The regime also became notorious for torture; the case of prisoners boiled to death was documented by the British ambassador at the time.

The economic dimension was autarkic. Karimov pursued import substitution, kept the currency non-convertible, maintained multiple exchange rates, and closed borders with neighbors. This produced a bizarre economy in which the official exchange rate was a fraction of the black-market rate, foreign companies could not repatriate profits, and cross-border trade with Kazakhstan or Kyrgyzstan involved smuggling through minefields. Uzbekistan sat at the center of Central Asia but was economically walled off from it. Millions of Uzbeks went to Russia as labor migrants, and their remittances became a major share of GDP.

The ideological dimension was national. Karimov promoted the "ideology of national independence," rehabilitated Timur as the founding father (a monumental statue replaced Marx in central Tashkent), sponsored the study of Jadids and Nava'i, and framed Uzbekistan as the ancient heir of Transoxianan greatness. This was the Soviet-manufactured nation now operating under new management, with the socialist content swapped for a nationalist one.

It is easy to condemn the Karimov system, and much of it deserves condemnation. The analytically interesting point is that it was coherent. It traded growth and freedom for stability, and it delivered stability, in a neighborhood where that was not guaranteed. The cost was a country that by 2016 was poor, isolated, corrupt, and fearful, with a generation of talent abroad.

## Concept Seven: The Mirziyoyev Opening

Shavkat Mirziyoyev, Karimov's long-serving prime minister, succeeded him in 2016 and surprised nearly everyone by launching a wide-ranging reform program. This is the period that makes Uzbekistan a live case in comparative politics rather than merely a historical one.

The economic reforms were real and fast. The currency was made convertible in 2017, collapsing the black-market spread overnight. Borders with neighbors were reopened, and regional trade surged. Visa requirements were lifted for most Western and Asian visitors, and tourism boomed. Foreign investment was courted, state enterprises were slated for privatization, and the cotton sector was reorganized into private "clusters" with the forced-labor system dismantled. Uzbekistan applied to join the World Trade Organization. The state released political prisoners, closed the notorious Jaslyk prison, loosened media controls, and allowed criticism of local officials that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier.

Regionally, Mirziyoyev reversed Karimov's isolation and positioned Uzbekistan as a convener. Border disputes with Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan were negotiated. Uzbekistan engaged with the Taliban after 2021, pushed for a trans-Afghan railway to reach Pakistani ports, and promoted a Central Asian regional identity. The strategic logic returns us to geography: a doubly-landlocked country can only prosper by becoming a hub, and a hub requires open borders and good relations with everyone.

The limits are equally important. The political system remains authoritarian. Mirziyoyev secured constitutional changes in 2023 that reset his term limits and extended presidential terms to seven years, a familiar move in the post-Soviet playbook. Elections are not competitive. When Karakalpakstan, the autonomous region in the northwest, erupted in protest in 2022 over a proposed constitutional change removing its right to secede, security forces killed around twenty people and the government backed down on the amendment while prosecuting the protesters. Corruption remains pervasive, and much of the new wealth flows to a connected elite. The reform model, in other words, is closer to authoritarian liberalization than democratization: open the economy, loosen daily repression, keep the presidency untouchable. Whether this equilibrium is stable, or whether economic opening generates political demands that the system cannot absorb, is precisely the question comparative political scientists are watching.

## Concept Eight: Society, the Mahalla, and Lived Islam

Beneath the state, Uzbek society runs on institutions that predate it.

The *mahalla* is the neighborhood community, a residential unit of a few hundred to a few thousand people bound by proximity, mutual obligation, and ritual life. Mahallas organize weddings and funerals, mediate disputes, maintain communal spaces, and enforce norms. The Soviet state co-opted them as instruments of administration; the Karimov state formalized them further and used them for surveillance and welfare distribution. Anthropologists debate whether the modern mahalla is authentic civil society or a state appendage wearing traditional dress; the honest answer is both, and the tension is productive to think about. If you want to understand why Uzbek social life feels dense and obligated in ways that surprise Westerners, the mahalla is the place to start.

Islam in Uzbekistan is overwhelmingly Sunni of the Hanafi school, historically shaped by Sufi orders, particularly the Naqshbandi, whose founder is buried outside Bukhara and whose shrine is a major pilgrimage site. Seventy years of Soviet secularization produced a population that is culturally and ritually Muslim, marking life-cycle events religiously, observing Ramadan fasts widely, and revering saints and shrines, while being generally relaxed about doctrinal observance and comfortable with a secular state. The post-independence revival has increased mosque attendance and visible piety, especially among the young, and the Mirziyoyev government has cautiously loosened restrictions on beards, hijab, and religious education while still keeping a firm hand. The state's model is what one might call managed Islam: religion as heritage and morality, monitored closely as politics.

Language is a quiet arena of nation-building. Uzbek, a Karluk Turkic language closely related to Uyghur, is the state language, and the script was switched from Cyrillic to Latin after independence, though the transition has been slow and partial, with older generations and much print media still using Cyrillic. Russian remains the language of business, higher education, and the urban middle class, and is the lingua franca with neighbors. Tajik persists in Samarkand and Bukhara. The gradual shift toward Uzbek and Latin script is a decades-long project of de-Sovietization pursued without the antagonism toward Russia seen in the Baltics or Ukraine.

Demographically, Uzbekistan is young, with a median age in the late twenties, and growing. This is the country's greatest asset and greatest challenge. A youth bulge can be a demographic dividend if the economy generates jobs and a source of instability if it does not, which is one reason the economic reforms have urgency beyond ideology.

## Concept Nine: Geopolitics of the Middle

Uzbekistan's foreign policy is best understood as *multi-vector balancing*, a term Central Asian states use for the practice of maintaining workable relations with all major powers and committing fully to none.

Russia is the historical hegemon, the destination for millions of labor migrants, a major trade partner, and the source of security guarantees Uzbekistan has been careful not to over-rely on. Uzbekistan joined and then left the Collective Security Treaty Organization twice, and has stayed out of the Eurasian Economic Union while taking observer status. Since the invasion of Ukraine, Tashkent has stated it does not recognize the separatist entities, has avoided sanctions violations conspicuously enough to satisfy the West, and has quietly benefited from Russian capital and businesses relocating.

China is the rising economic partner, the largest source of investment, the market for gas, and the builder of infrastructure through the Belt and Road Initiative. The China-Kyrgyzstan-Uzbekistan railway, long delayed, is the flagship project that would give Uzbekistan a direct rail link to China bypassing Kazakhstan and Russia.

The United States and Europe are sources of investment, technology, education, and legitimacy, and their interest in the region rises and falls with Afghanistan. Turkey offers a pan-Turkic cultural connection through the Organization of Turkic States. The Gulf states bring capital. South Korea, unusually, has deep ties through the ethnic Korean population deported to Central Asia by Stalin in 1937 and through decades of investment; Daewoo built Uzbekistan's automotive industry.

The conceptual core is that Uzbekistan has no ocean, no defensible frontier, and neighbors on every side, so it cannot afford enemies. Its strategic ambition is to convert location back into leverage, as it was in the caravan age, by becoming the place through which everyone's goods and interests must pass.

## Synthesis: How to Think About Uzbekistan

Pull the threads together and a set of tensions emerges that define the country.

There is the tension between territorial heritage and ethnic origin: a Turkic nation claiming a Persianate civilization, with Timur as founder and Nava'i as poet and Bukhara as holy city, all of it assembled into coherent national narrative within living memory.

There is the tension between centrality and isolation: a place that was the crossroads of the world, then its backwater, now attempting to become a crossroads again through railways and open borders rather than camels.

There is the tension between the Soviet inheritance and its repudiation: a state built by Soviet nationality policy, run by Soviet-trained elites, using Soviet institutions, while officially narrating itself as the victim of Soviet colonialism.

There is the tension between reform and control: an authoritarian system that has genuinely liberalized its economy and daily life while preserving the presidency as an unaccountable center, betting that prosperity will substitute for participation.

And beneath all of it there is water: the rivers that made civilization possible, the cotton that drank them dry, the dead sea in the northwest, and the glaciers upstream that climate change is now melting on a schedule no one controls.

A beginner should leave this lesson able to place Uzbekistan on a map, name its major cities and their significance, explain why cotton and the Aral Sea matter, and describe the difference between the Karimov and Mirziyoyev eras. A specialist should leave it with sharper questions: whether Soviet nation-building produced identities that will prove as durable as they now seem, whether authoritarian liberalization has an endpoint, whether connectivity can actually reverse the geographic marginalization of the last five centuries, and whether a country that lost a sea can adapt to losing its glaciers. Those questions do not have settled answers, which is exactly what makes the place worth studying.
