# At Least These
The harbourmaster sells diesel from a pump at the end of the quay, and nailed to the post beside it is a list: a radio; lifejackets, one for everyone aboard; a light that works. A boat that comes alongside is checked against the list, and if the three things are there she is fuelled. There is no paperwork.

A trawler comes in first — radio, lifejackets, light, and also nets, a winch, a hold of ice and a smell that stays after she has gone. Fuel. Then a yacht with the three things and a dishwasher. The dishwasher is not on the list and is not held against it. The list says what a boat must have aboard, and nothing about what else she may carry: at least these, not only these.

Third is the lifeboat, built in 1975 by a yard that closed before the list existed. Nobody who built her ever saw it, and she was not built to be a fuel boat: nothing is registered, and there is no book in the office with the names of the boats that qualify. The harbourmaster does not ask what she is called or what she is for; he looks for a radio, lifejackets and a light, finds them, and fills her.

Read the list again, not as three lines but as the boats it lets through: every boat afloat with at least those three things aboard, whatever else she carries and whoever built her. The list is a type; the boats are values. A type is a set of possible values: string | number is every string and every number, two sets put together; never is the empty set; unknown is everything; and an object type is the set of all objects that have at least those properties — which is why shape, not name, decides what fits.

A second list, on the office door, is for boats staying the night: the same three things and an anchor. Every boat that may stay already qualifies for fuel; most boats at the pump may not stay. The longer the list, the fewer the boats, and the smaller set lies entirely inside the larger: an overnight boat can be sent to the pump without a second look, and not the reverse.

Across the water the yacht club has its own pump and its own board, under its own heading, and it happens to list the same three things. A boat passed at one pump is passed at the other, and nobody telephones to confirm. Two headings, one set of boats.

On the harbour plan there is a numbered berth for boats under four metres long and over thirty. Nothing has ever lain in it, because the two conditions together pick out no boat at all. That is never. Whatever is tied up there may be moved to any berth in the harbour, which the harbourmaster grants freely, since it costs nothing.

Outside the wall is an anchorage, opened in 2018, where anything that floats may lie. Everything is admitted to it and nothing is admitted from it. A boat out there gets no fuel and no berth until someone has rowed out and read the list against her; until then the harbour knows one thing about her: that she is out there. That is unknown.

On the harbourmaster's day off the pump is left running and the list is not read. A boat that comes in that day is fuelled, given whatever berth she asks for, and treated from then on as whatever she was taken for, by people who looked at nothing. That is any, and it is not a set; it is a decision not to look. The one place even that boat cannot go is the berth for boats under four metres and over thirty.

One exception looks at first like a contradiction of the yacht. A boat being fitted out at the pump — the owner reading the list off the post and bolting things on to meet it — is checked for what is not on the list as well. If the owner paints "lifejakets" on a locker, the harbourmaster says: did you mean lifejackets? The yacht's dishwasher was fitted elsewhere, for other reasons. A boat fitted out to the list has no reason to carry anything the list did not ask for, so anything extra is most likely a word spelled wrong. TypeScript's word for a boat in that state, in the notes for version 1.6 in September 2015, is fresh. The moment she leaves the pump she is a boat like any other, and may come back with a dishwasher.

The list was nailed to the post in 2015. The lifeboat had been on it for forty years. 1.
