# Semiconductor Physics, Contracted

*A lesson that begins with no prerequisites and ends within sight of a qualifying exam. Each section opens with the picture and then goes deeper; on a first pass, it's fine to skim the second half of each. Two units recur throughout: the electron-volt (eV), the energy an electron gains falling through one volt, and kT, the typical thermal energy, which at room temperature is 26 meV — a number worth tattooing somewhere.*

## 0. The whole subject in one paragraph

A semiconductor is a crystal whose electrons are forbidden from having energies inside a window — the band gap — that is small enough (roughly 0.1 to 4 eV) that heat, chemical doping, light, or an applied voltage can move a controllable number of electrons across it or around it. Every device downstream — diode, transistor, LED, laser, solar cell, image sensor — is an exercise in controlling where the Fermi level sits relative to that gap, as a function of position and time. Internalize that sentence and the rest is detail. Extremely consequential detail.

## 1. Where bands and gaps come from

An isolated atom has discrete energy levels. Bring 10²³ atoms together into a crystal and each level splits into 10²³ sub-levels so finely spaced they form a continuum — a band. But the band that grows out of one atomic level need not overlap the band that grows out of the next, and the energy intervals in between, where no electron state exists at all, are the gaps. That is the chemist's picture: linear combinations of atomic orbitals, or tight-binding. The physicist's picture starts from the opposite limit, a free electron wave moving through a periodic potential. Bloch's theorem says the stationary states are plane waves modulated by a lattice-periodic function, ψ_k(r) = e^{ik·r} u_k(r), labeled by a "crystal momentum" k that lives in a finite region called the Brillouin zone. At the zone boundary the electron wave is Bragg-reflected by the lattice; the forward and backward waves combine into two standing waves, one piling charge onto the ion cores (lower energy) and one piling it between them (higher energy), and the energy difference between those two is a gap. Both pictures converge on the same object, the band structure E(k), which for silicon or gallium arsenide is a plot you will stare at for years.

Now fill it. Each band holds two electrons (spin up, spin down) per primitive cell. Silicon has two atoms per primitive cell and four valence electrons each: eight electrons, exactly filling four bands — the sp³ bonding states, collectively called the valence band. The antibonding states above, the conduction band, are empty at absolute zero. A completely full band carries no current, for a reason deeper than "no room to move": the velocities of all states in a band sum to exactly zero by symmetry, and an electric field cannot change the occupation of a band that has no empty states to shift into. Full valence band plus empty conduction band equals insulator. Metals have partially filled bands. A semiconductor is simply an insulator whose gap is small enough to be interesting: Ge 0.66 eV, Si 1.12 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV, 4H-SiC 3.26 eV, GaN 3.4 eV, diamond 5.5 eV, all at room temperature. The line between semiconductor and insulator is drawn by engineering, not physics — diamond became a semiconductor when we learned to dope it.

One geometric feature of E(k) matters enormously. In GaAs the conduction-band minimum sits directly above the valence-band maximum at k = 0 (the Γ point): a direct gap. In silicon the conduction-band minima sit at six equivalent points about 85% of the way to the zone boundary along the ⟨100⟩ directions, while the valence maximum stays at Γ: an indirect gap. Germanium's minima are at the L points along ⟨111⟩. Because a photon carries negligible momentum on the scale of a Brillouin zone, this single fact decides which materials emit light (Section 9), and it shapes effective masses, scattering, and even the strain tricks inside a modern CPU.

## 2. Quasiparticles: effective mass and holes

Near a band extremum, E(k) is smooth, so expand it: E ≈ E_c + ħ²k²/2m*. The curvature defines an effective mass m*, and the small miracle of semiclassical theory is that a wavepacket built from Bloch states responds to external forces as F = m* dv/dt, with the crystal's entire periodic potential absorbed into that one number. Flat bands mean heavy, sluggish carriers; sharply curved bands mean light, fast ones. In general m* is a tensor: silicon's six conduction valleys are ellipsoids with a longitudinal mass near 0.9 m₀ and a transverse mass of 0.19 m₀, which is why silicon's "electron mass" depends on what you're computing (about 1.08 m₀ for counting states, 0.26 m₀ for conductivity). GaAs electrons have m* = 0.067 m₀ in a single isotropic valley, one reason its mobility is six times silicon's.

Why is m* what it is? Here is the graduate-level answer, from k·p perturbation theory: the curvature of the conduction band is dominated by its quantum-mechanical repulsion from the valence band across the gap, with a strength set by an interband momentum matrix element, E_P ≈ 20–30 eV, that is nearly universal across the tetrahedral semiconductors. The result, m*/m₀ ≈ E_g/E_P, says narrow-gap materials must have light electrons. InSb, with a 0.17 eV gap, has m* = 0.014 m₀ and room-temperature mobility near 80,000 cm²/V·s; GaN, with 3.4 eV, has m* ≈ 0.2 m₀. Gap and mass are not independent knobs.

Now the valence band, which is nearly full. Remove one electron. The current carried by the remaining 10²³ − 1 electrons is exactly minus the current the missing one would have carried, and the response of that whole ensemble to a field is exactly that of a single particle with charge +e and a positive mass equal to the magnitude of the (negative) curvature at the valence-band top. That particle is the hole. It is not a bookkeeping fiction; it is as real as a phonon, and every statement about a hole is an exact statement about the collective motion of a full band minus one. Because the valence-band top in the cubic semiconductors is built from p-like orbitals, it comes in three flavors: heavy holes and light holes, degenerate at Γ, plus a split-off band pushed down by spin–orbit coupling (44 meV in Si, 340 meV in GaAs). Strain lifts the heavy/light degeneracy and lets you engineer the hole mass, which is precisely what the SiGe stressors in a p-channel transistor do.

## 3. Counting carriers: statistics and the Fermi level

How many electrons occupy the conduction band at temperature T? Multiply the density of available states, which in three dimensions rises as g(E) ∝ √(E − E_c), by the probability each is occupied, the Fermi–Dirac function f(E) = 1/[1 + exp((E − E_F)/kT)], and integrate. The Fermi level E_F is the chemical potential of the electrons — the energy at which a state would be half-occupied, whether or not any state actually exists there — and it is the single most important quantity in the subject. When E_F sits in the gap, several kT from either band edge, the Fermi function collapses to a Boltzmann tail and the integrals give n = N_c exp[−(E_c − E_F)/kT] and p = N_v exp[−(E_F − E_v)/kT], where N_c and N_v, the effective densities of states (about 3×10¹⁹ and 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in silicon at 300 K), are effectively the number of states within a kT of the band edges. Multiply the two and the Fermi level cancels: np = N_c N_v exp(−E_g/kT) ≡ n_i². This is the law of mass action, and it is exactly what a chemist would write for the equilibrium of the reaction "nothing ⇌ electron + hole": the electron–hole product is fixed by temperature and gap alone, whatever you do to the individual concentrations.

The numbers should shock you. For silicon at room temperature n_i ≈ 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, against 5×10²² atoms per cm³: one thermally excited pair per five trillion atoms. Pure silicon is nearly an insulator, and because n_i goes as exp(−E_g/2kT) it roughly doubles every 9 °C. Germanium, with half the gap, has n_i ≈ 2×10¹³ and leaks accordingly; GaN has n_i ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ cm⁻³, meaning a cubic centimeter of intrinsic GaN at equilibrium contains, on average, no free carrier at all. When doping pushes E_F into a band the Boltzmann shortcut fails, you need full Fermi–Dirac integrals, and the material is "degenerate": it behaves like a poor metal with a Fermi surface, and n stops growing exponentially with E_F. Degeneracy is where semiconductor physics hands off to metal physics.

## 4. Doping: the hydrogen atom, rescaled

Replace one silicon atom in ten million with phosphorus. Phosphorus brings five valence electrons; four go into bonds, and the fifth orbits a net +e at the phosphorus core, screened by the crystal's dielectric constant and moving with the conduction-band effective mass. That is a hydrogen atom with e² → e²/ε_r and m₀ → m*, so its binding energy is 13.6 eV × (m*/m₀)/ε_r² — roughly 25–30 meV in silicon, 5.5 meV in GaAs — and its Bohr radius is 0.053 nm × ε_r/(m*/m₀), about 2–3 nm in silicon and 10 nm in GaAs, spanning hundreds to thousands of lattice sites. That large radius is what justifies treating the crystal as a continuum with a dielectric constant and an effective mass in the first place; the discrepancy between the ~30 meV estimate and the measured 45 meV for P in Si is the "central-cell correction," the actual chemistry inside the first shell that the continuum can't see, and it is why P, As, and Sb sit at slightly different energies. Since kT at room temperature is 26 meV, shallow donors are essentially all ionized: each contributes one conduction electron and E_F moves up toward E_c. Boron, with three valence electrons, accepts an electron from the valence band and leaves a hole: p-type, E_F moves down. Donors and acceptors present together compensate — only the net matters for the carrier count, but both count as scatterers.

Mass action now does something remarkable. Dope silicon n-type at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ (one atom in five million) and n = 10¹⁶ while p = n_i²/n = 10⁴ cm⁻³ — the hole population is suppressed by twelve orders of magnitude. These minority carriers are almost absent, and yet the entire operation of diodes, bipolar transistors, and solar cells rides on them, because they are the population whose concentration a single volt can change by a factor of a billion. Doping has a temperature story too: at cryogenic temperatures carriers freeze back onto their dopants; across a wide plateau n ≈ N_D independent of T (the regime devices are designed for); and at high enough temperature n_i overtakes N_D and the material forgets it was doped, which is why silicon electronics dies above ~150–200 °C and SiC and GaN carry on. Push the doping toward 10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and the donor wavefunctions overlap, an impurity band forms, and the material crosses a Mott metal–insulator transition (near 4×10¹⁸ cm⁻³ for Si:P) into a degenerate, metallic state; many-body effects also shrink the gap by tens of meV. Not all impurities are shallow, either. Gold, iron, or a dangling bond creates a deep level near midgap that binds carriers by hundreds of meV; deep levels are nearly useless for supplying carriers and superbly effective at killing them (Section 6).

## 5. Transport: drift, diffusion, and what scatters what

A carrier in a field accelerates, scatters, and on average drifts at v = μE, with mobility μ = eτ/m*, where τ is the momentum relaxation time, a few tenths of a picosecond in silicon. Conductivity is σ = e(nμ_n + pμ_p), and since n can be dialed from 10¹⁰ to 10²⁰ cm⁻³, so can σ, across ten decades — the defining property of the material class. What sets τ? Lattice vibrations, phonons, act as a moving corrugation: acoustic phonons scatter at a rate growing as T^{3/2} (so μ ∝ T^{−3/2}), while optical phonons — polar and violent in the III–Vs — take over at higher carrier energies. Ionized dopants deflect carriers by Coulomb scattering, less effectively the faster the carrier, so μ_imp ∝ T^{3/2}/N_I: heavier doping buys conductivity at the price of mobility. Rates add (Matthiessen's rule, 1/μ = Σ 1/μ_i), so phonons win at room temperature and cooling helps only until the impurities take over. Modulation doping breaks that trade: park the donors in an AlGaAs barrier and let their electrons fall into an adjacent GaAs layer, spatially separated from the ions that donated them, and the low-temperature mobility climbs past 10⁷ cm²/V·s — the platform on which the fractional quantum Hall effect was discovered and where its stranger relatives are still hunted. The rigorous machinery behind all this is the Boltzmann transport equation, with scattering rates from Fermi's golden rule, 1/τ = (2π/ħ)|M|²g(E), weighted by (1 − cos θ) because only deflection, not forward scattering, relaxes momentum. The Hall effect, whose coefficient is 1/ne, is how you measure n and the sign of the carriers; the Seebeck coefficient, essentially the entropy per carrier, is why moderately doped semiconductors make the best thermoelectrics.

Fields strong enough to heat carriers faster than phonons can cool them break the linear law: electrons in silicon saturate near 10⁷ cm/s, the speed at which each increment of energy gained from the field is promptly dumped into a 63 meV optical phonon. In GaAs, hot electrons scatter from the light Γ valley into heavy satellite valleys 0.3 eV higher, velocity falls as the field rises, and the resulting negative differential resistance makes microwaves (the Gunn effect). Meanwhile concentration gradients drive diffusion, J = eD∇n, and the Einstein relation D = μkT/e — a fluctuation–dissipation statement, since the same scattering that limits drift drives the random walk — ties the two together. The elegant unification: the total electron current is J_n = nμ_n∇E_Fn. Drift and diffusion are one phenomenon, the flow of carriers down the gradient of their electrochemical potential. That is why the Fermi level is flat through any structure in equilibrium — it must be, or current would flow — and it is the rule that lets you draw every band diagram in this subject. Add Poisson's equation for the electrostatics and a continuity equation for each carrier, and you have the drift–diffusion system every TCAD simulator solves. The length over which mobile carriers screen a charge is the Debye length (tens of nanometers at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³) and the time is the dielectric relaxation time ε/σ, femtoseconds to picoseconds in doped material.

## 6. Out of equilibrium: recombination and quasi-Fermi levels

Shine light or inject current and np exceeds n_i². The system relaxes through three channels. Radiative recombination — an electron falls into a hole and emits a photon, rate B·np — is fast in direct-gap materials (B ~ 10⁻¹⁰ cm³/s in GaAs) and painfully slow in silicon. Shockley–Read–Hall recombination proceeds through deep levels: a trap captures an electron and then a hole, in two steps, neither needing momentum conservation; it dominates in silicon, with a lifetime τ set by trap density, from nanoseconds in dirty material to milliseconds in the float-zone silicon of a record solar cell. Auger recombination is the three-body process in which the recombination energy is handed to a third carrier instead of a photon, rate ~ Cn³; it is intrinsic, unavoidable, and dominant at high carrier density, which is why it sets the ultimate efficiency of silicon photovoltaics and causes the efficiency droop of GaN LEDs at high current. Surfaces, with their dangling bonds, are dense sheets of deep levels, and the art of passivation — hydrogen, thermal oxide, Al₂O₃ with its fixed negative charge — is the art of shutting them up.

Lifetime and diffusion coefficient combine into the minority-carrier diffusion length L = √(Dτ), the distance an injected carrier wanders before it dies. For electrons in good silicon, D ≈ 36 cm²/s and τ ≈ 1 ms give L ≈ 2 mm, longer than the wafer is thick, and that is the whole reason an indirect-gap material can be a 27%-efficient solar cell: carriers generated deep in the bulk survive long enough to reach the junction. To describe a driven population thermodynamically, split the Fermi level in two. Electrons thermalize among themselves in well under a picosecond and settle into their own quasi-Fermi level E_Fn, holes into E_Fp, and np = n_i² exp[(E_Fn − E_Fp)/kT]. The splitting is the free energy stored in the excited crystal: it is the open-circuit voltage of a solar cell, the applied voltage across a forward-biased diode, and, once it exceeds the photon energy, the condition for laser gain.

## 7. Junctions

Bring p-type and n-type silicon into contact. The Fermi level must be flat in equilibrium, but it sits near E_v on one side and near E_c on the other, so the bands must bend by a total of eV_bi = kT ln(N_A N_D/n_i²), about 0.7–0.9 V in silicon. Physically, electrons diffuse from n to p and holes from p to n, leaving behind immobile charged dopant ions; that space charge produces exactly the field needed to halt further diffusion. The region swept nearly clear of mobile carriers is the depletion region; the depletion approximation gives its width as W = √[2ε(V_bi − V)(1/N_A + 1/N_D)/e], sub-micron at typical doping, and its capacitance is that of a parallel plate of separation W, which is how C–V measurements profile doping. Now apply a forward bias V: the barrier drops by eV and the number of carriers with enough energy to cross it — a Boltzmann tail — rises by exp(eV/kT). At the edges of the depletion region the minority concentration is boosted to its equilibrium value times exp(eV/kT) (the "law of the junction," which is just Section 6's quasi-Fermi-level statement applied locally); the injected minority carriers diffuse away and recombine within a diffusion length, and the resulting current is Shockley's I = I₀[exp(eV/nkT) − 1]. Under reverse bias the exponential vanishes and what remains, I₀, is the trickle of thermally generated minority carriers within a diffusion length of the junction being swept across — tiny, nearly independent of voltage, and scaling as n_i or n_i² depending on whether generation inside the depletion region or diffusion outside it dominates, which is why leakage is so ferociously temperature-sensitive. The famous "0.7 V turn-on" is not a threshold; it is where an exponential spanning twelve decades becomes visible on a linear plot. The ideality factor n drifts from 1 toward 2 as depletion-region recombination takes over from diffusion. Push the reverse bias far enough and the diode breaks down, by impact-ionization avalanche in lightly doped junctions or by interband Zener tunneling in heavily doped ones, both reversibly if the current is limited.

A metal on a semiconductor should, by the Schottky–Mott rule, form a barrier equal to the metal's work function minus the semiconductor's electron affinity, but real interfaces pin the Fermi level with a dense set of interface states (in covalent semiconductors, metal-induced gap states) and barrier heights depend only weakly on the metal — one of the field's long-running annoyances. A Schottky diode conducts by majority carriers thermionically emitted over the barrier, which makes it fast; an ohmic contact is a Schottky barrier made so thin by degenerate doping that carriers tunnel straight through. Heterojunctions between different semiconductors, grown epitaxially, turn the band offset at the interface into a design variable: with it you can confine electrons in a well, make a bipolar transistor's emitter inject preferentially, or, as in the HEMT amplifying your phone's signal, pile a two-dimensional electron gas against a barrier. The bipolar transistor itself is two junctions back-to-back sharing a thin base: the forward-biased emitter junction injects minority carriers into the base, nearly all of them diffuse across before recombining and are swept out by the reverse-biased collector, and a small base current thereby controls a large collector current, with a gain set largely by how thin the base is compared with the diffusion length.

## 8. The MOS system and the transistor

Now the device that made the modern world. A gate, a thin insulator, and p-type silicon form a capacitor. A negative gate voltage pulls holes to the surface (accumulation). A positive one repels them, exposes depleted acceptor charge, and bends the bands down at the surface; keep going and the conduction-band edge at the surface approaches E_F, so electrons — minority carriers, in a p-type crystal — begin to gather there. When the surface potential reaches twice the bulk Fermi potential, the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density and a conducting sheet, quantum-mechanically a two-dimensional electron gas a few nanometers thick, has been conjured by electrostatics alone: inversion. The gate voltage at which this happens is the threshold, V_T. Add n-type source and drain contacts to that sheet and you have a MOSFET. Above threshold the channel holds C_ox(V_G − V_T) of charge per unit area and the current is roughly I_D = μC_ox(W/L)[(V_G − V_T)V_D − V_D²/2], saturating when the drain end of the channel pinches off at V_D = V_G − V_T. Below threshold the channel charge is a Boltzmann tail, so the current falls by a factor of e for every kT/e of gate voltage — at best 60 mV per decade at room temperature. This "Boltzmann tyranny" is a thermodynamic floor, not an engineering one: it is why supply voltages stalled near 0.7 V, why leakage power became the binding constraint on chips, and why tunnel FETs and negative-capacitance FETs are studied as ways to cheat it.

Scaling the transistor has been a series of confrontations with the physics above. Below about 2 nm of SiO₂ the gate leaked by direct tunneling, so hafnium-based high-κ dielectrics replaced it. Mobility fell in thin channels, so strain — tensile for electrons, to depopulate the heavy-mass valleys; compressive for holes, to split heavy from light — was engineered into every channel. Short channels let the drain's field reach the source and lower the barrier (drain-induced barrier lowering), ruining the off-state, so the gate was wrapped around a fin, then around a nanosheet, to reassert electrostatic control. In a 15 nm channel with a mean free path of 10–20 nm, a large fraction of electrons cross without scattering at all, and the current is better described by the injection velocity at the source-side barrier than by a mobility — the quasi-ballistic regime, where drift–diffusion is patched with quantum corrections or replaced outright by non-equilibrium Green's function simulation.

## 9. Light

A photon with energy above the gap can lift an electron from the valence to the conduction band. Because the photon's momentum is negligible the transition is vertical in k, and in a direct-gap material the absorption coefficient rises as √(ħω − E_g) to 10⁴–10⁵ cm⁻¹: a few microns of GaAs absorb most of the sunlight they can use. In silicon the transition needs a phonon to supply the momentum mismatch, so absorption near the gap is a second-order process and weak, about 10² cm⁻¹ at 1 µm wavelength; you need a hundred microns of silicon, or clever light-trapping textures, to catch the red end of the spectrum. The gap also explains color: a crystal is transparent below its gap, so GaN is clear, CdS is yellow, and GaAs and silicon look metallic gray because they swallow the entire visible range. Emission is the time-reverse of absorption, so LEDs and lasers live in direct-gap materials — GaAs and its alloys in the red and infrared, InGaN on GaN in the blue and green, the achievement behind the 2014 Nobel Prize and the collapse in the cost of white light. A laser additionally needs gain, which requires population inversion; in a semiconductor the condition is E_Fn − E_Fp > ħω > E_g (the Bernard–Duraffourg inequality), reached by driving a junction hard enough, and reached at far lower current when the active region is a quantum well whose step-like density of states concentrates carriers at the band edge. A solar cell is the same junction run backwards: photogenerated pairs are separated by the built-in field, and the single-junction limit — Shockley and Queisser's 33% for a gap near 1.3 eV, from the trade-off between sub-gap photons lost and above-gap energy thermalized to heat — is beaten only by stacking gaps in a multijunction. Just below the gap, light can still create excitons, bound electron–hole pairs that are once again hydrogen atoms rescaled: 4 meV in GaAs, 15 meV in silicon, relevant mostly when cold, but hundreds of meV in atomically thin MoS₂ or WS₂, where weak screening makes them the dominant optical feature even at room temperature.

## 10. Small, cold, and coherent

Shrink a semiconductor to the electron's de Broglie wavelength — tens of nanometers, thanks to the small m* — and quantization becomes a design parameter. A quantum well of width L has subbands at E_n = ħ²π²n²/2m*L² and a density of states that is a staircase rather than a square root; a wire gives inverse-square-root spikes; a dot gives discrete atom-like levels, which is why quantum dots are called artificial atoms and why their emission color is set by their diameter. Tunneling, exponentially sensitive to barrier width and height through the WKB integral, is the mechanism of the Esaki diode, of resonant-tunneling structures, of gate leakage, and of the Fowler–Nordheim programming of NAND flash. When a conductor is shorter than the mean free path and phase-coherent, conductance is no longer σA/L but a sum over transmitting modes, G = (2e²/h)Σ T_n — Landauer's formula — so a narrow constriction in a 2DEG conducts in integer steps of 2e²/h ≈ 77.5 µS. Put that 2DEG in a strong magnetic field and the Hall resistance locks onto h/νe² to a part in 10¹⁰, the quantum Hall effect, now the practical realization of the ohm, with fractional ν revealing that strongly interacting electrons can form quasiparticles of fractional charge. Isolate a dot so small that adding one electron costs more than kT in charging energy and you have Coulomb blockade, the single-electron transistor, and, in isotopically purified silicon, spin qubits that are now serious contenders for quantum computing.

## 11. How it is actually computed

Band structures come from density functional theory, which gets structure and bonding right but systematically underestimates gaps (the local-density approximation gives ~0.5 eV for silicon) because Kohn–Sham eigenvalues are not quasiparticle energies; the GW approximation restores them. Empirical pseudopotentials and tight-binding fit the known bands and are cheap enough for million-atom devices; k·p, with a handful of parameters, delivers the band edges, masses, and strain response that device physics needs. Transport is done by drift–diffusion in TCAD for anything larger than about 100 nm, by Monte Carlo solution of the Boltzmann equation when carriers are hot, and by non-equilibrium Green's functions when the channel is quantum. Knowing which tool is valid at which length and energy scale is most of the craft.

## 12. The zoo and the frontier

Silicon won for its oxide and its abundance, not for its physics. The wide-gap materials, SiC and GaN, withstand fields roughly ten times higher (breakdown field grows steeply with gap) and so build power converters that are smaller, faster, and less lossy — the electronics inside electric vehicles and fast chargers. Gallium oxide and diamond push the gap further and are held back, respectively, by the absence of p-type doping and the lack of shallow dopants. At the other extreme, HgCdTe with its tunable near-zero gap sees in the thermal infrared. Graphene has no gap and no mass — its carriers obey a Dirac equation with velocity 10⁶ m/s — which makes it a superb conductor and a hopeless switch; monolayer transition-metal dichalcogenides restore a direct gap plus a valley degree of freedom addressable with circularly polarized light. Halide perovskites tolerate defects that ought to kill them and went from curiosity to 26% solar cells in a decade. Oxide semiconductors such as IGZO drive the display you may be reading this on. Organic semiconductors conduct by hopping between molecules and need a different transport theory entirely. And topological insulators carry current in symmetry-protected edge states — a reminder that a band structure encodes not only energies but the geometry of the wavefunctions.

## Coda: the compressed version of the compressed version

Semiconductor physics is governed by a handful of dimensionless comparisons. The gap against kT decides whether the material conducts on its own. Doping against n_i decides whether you or the temperature is in charge. Device size against the diffusion length decides whether minority carriers survive the trip; against the Debye length, whether space charge matters; against the mean free path, whether mobility means anything; against the de Broglie wavelength, whether you need quantum mechanics beyond the effective mass. Running through all of it is the Fermi level — flat in equilibrium, split when driven — whose position relative to the band edges, set by chemistry, geometry, voltage, and light, is what every semiconductor device manipulates. Master the band diagram and where E_F sits on it and you can reason your way to almost any device without a formula. The numbers worth carrying in your head are kT = 26 meV, silicon's gap of 1.12 eV, n_i ≈ 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, N_c ≈ 3×10¹⁹, μ_n ≈ 1400 cm²/V·s and D_n ≈ 36 cm²/s, 60 mV per decade, and 2e²/h ≈ 77.5 µS.
